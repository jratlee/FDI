What It Actually Took to Build Pile: A Founder's Update
A build-in-public update from False Dawn Industries — product, marketing, and content, with the honest parts left in.

I shipped a pay-per-question answer engine solo. The product was maybe a third of the actual work.

That's the update in one line. Here's the longer, honest version — what building this actually looked like across the three tracks nobody warns you run in parallel: the product, the marketing, and the content.

The origin, briefly
For two years I hoarded good answers. Every time I dug something worth keeping out of a report, a book, or a paper I trusted, it went into one growing folder. Friends started asking me to "look it up in your pile." That was the tell: the value wasn't the folder, it was being able to ask it a question and get one clear answer back.

Pile is that idea shipped as software. You ask a question, you see a free preview of the answer, and you pay only if it earns it — $0.99 for a Quick answer, $1.99 for a Deep one. Every paid answer comes with its sources and a proof-of-delivery receipt.

Simple pitch. Now the part nobody puts in the pitch.

Track 1: Product — the last 20% was 80% of the work
The core is retrieval-augmented generation: documents synced from a private Drive folder, chunked, embedded, stored as vectors in Postgres, searched by similarity, answered by a model with citations. A competent weekend gets you a demo of that.

What the demo doesn't include:

Trust engineering. Preview-before-pay means the full answer is generated up front and held server-side until payment — which means the payment flow, the preview expiry, and the answer storage all have to agree, always. Add a proof-of-delivery hash, a dispute flow, and a quality check that warns buyers when confidence is low. Trust isn't a feature; it's a dozen small promises that all have to hold at once.

Money correctness. Stripe webhooks get redelivered. Payments race. I had to prove — with tests, not hope — that a buyer can never be credited twice for the same payment, that a duplicate webhook can't re-reset a subscriber's usage, and that a failed billing update never silently vanishes. Nobody sees this work. Everybody would see its absence.

Privacy as a moving target. The expert's source filenames and full document text must never leak to buyers. I locked that down on the main answer flow — and then had to lock it down again, separately, on every new surface I added, because each one (the API, the paid answer page, the admin routes) starts out knowing more than it should show. The lesson: source protection isn't a fix, it's a property every new surface has to re-earn. Same with logging: customer emails and question text kept creeping back into logs until I built guards that catch the regression before it ships.

Sync reliability. A knowledge business dies quietly if the knowledge goes stale. So: scheduled syncs that survive a serverless environment that scales to zero, failure alerts that reach the admin without spamming them daily, and a guarantee that a sync error never silently drops working knowledge.

There's also a Pro tier — API access and a machine-readable interface so AI agents can query the pile directly. It's built and tested, and deliberately switched off. Coming soon, on purpose: pay-per-question needs to prove itself first.

Track 2: Marketing — the rebrand tax is real
I renamed the product mid-build. Worth it — but the old name survived in browser tabs, social share captions, ad copy, and auth screens for weeks. Every rename you think is done, isn't. I ended up building automated checks that fail the build if the brand name or prices drift out of sync in ad creative, because "silently stale marketing" turned out to be a recurring bug class, not a one-off.

The rest of the marketing track: a full brand kit (warm editorial — forest green, cream, ink), ad creatives across Instagram, Facebook, Google, and TikTok sizes with A/B headline variants, Story frames built to survive real platform UI safe zones, and a five-part SEO pass covering crawlability, performance, metadata, internal linking, and structured data — including making the site legible to AI crawlers, because the next buyer might not be human.

Honest note: the wrong price shipped on a social share image twice before the automated check existed. Manual vigilance doesn't scale, even at a team size of one.

Track 3: Content — the product is also the proof
Three pieces so far: a launch content package (LinkedIn + Instagram, with a real calendar and tested hook variants), a technical build deep-dive for engineers, and a manifesto arguing that marketers should treat knowledge and identity as infrastructure machines can query.

The content strategy is simple: Pile is the argument. Every piece points at the same idea — owned, provable knowledge that people and machines can transact with — and the product is the working example. Content that isn't downstream of something real is just noise; I built the real thing first so the writing has something to stand on.

What I'd tell another founder
The demo is 20% of the product. Budget for the money correctness, the privacy regressions, and the reliability work — that's where the actual product lives.
Turn recurring mistakes into automated checks. If it broke twice, it's a bug class. Stop fixing instances.
Run all three tracks from day one. Product without marketing is invisible. Marketing without content is hollow. Content without product is noise.
Ship the small honest thing. Pile is deliberately small. It's also real, running, and hardened — which beats a big roadmap every time.
