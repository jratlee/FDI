# Aggregated: The Shift to AI-Mediated Discovery and the Case for Owned, Machine-Retrievable Identity

**Research Date:** July 5, 2026 (extended July 13, 2026 with the "marketing within today's platforms" half)
**Depth:** Standard (5 parallel focus areas, gap-checked; +4 platform-half focus areas)
**Sources Consulted:** 43
**Prepared for:** False Dawn Industries — Growth Cartography series, Field Guide 002 ("Aggregated")

---

## Executive Summary

Discovery is moving behind recommenders and chat assistants. A growing share of customers now ask a model instead of a search box, and the model, not the customer, decides which brands and sources are surfaced. The evidence for this shift is no longer speculative. Pew Research found that 58% of U.S. adults ran at least one Google search that returned an AI-generated summary in a single month, that users click links less often when a summary is present, and that only about 1% clicked a source cited inside the summary [1]. Bain & Company found roughly 80% of search users now rely on AI-written summaries for at least 40% of their searches, that about 60% of searches end without a click to any website, and that this is cutting organic web traffic by an estimated 15%–25% [2][3]. The consequence is a new discovery layer that sits between brands and their customers and that brands cannot buy their way into the way they bought impressions.

This is Aggregation Theory playing out one layer higher [13]. Aggregators win by owning the demand side of a market at zero marginal distribution cost, which commoditizes the suppliers behind them. AI answer engines are the newest and most powerful aggregators: they intermediate the customer relationship, absorb the click, and reduce every brand and publisher to an interchangeable input. The measurable damage is already visible. Global publisher traffic from Google fell roughly a third in the year to November 2025 [8], and both Chegg [10] and Penske Media (Rolling Stone, Variety, Billboard) [11][12] have sued Google, with Penske alleging a coercive bargain: allow your content into AI Overviews or accept worse rankings.

The AI answer layer is the newest chapter of a story marketers already know by heart, because the pre-AI half of the aggregated market tells the same story with two more decades of data. Three platforms (Meta, Google, Amazon) are forecast to absorb 62.3% of all worldwide digital ad spending in 2026, with eMarketer projecting Meta ($243.46B, 26.8% share) to pass Google ($239.54B, 26.4%) in ad revenue for the first time [35][36]. Inside those platforms, organic distribution has effectively collapsed as a strategy: the average Facebook page post reaches about 1.65% of its followers organically, Instagram about 3.50% [39], and on TikTok 85.1% of views arrive through the algorithmic For You page while the feed of accounts a user actually follows delivers roughly 0.3% [40]. Paid access compounds in cost: Meta's average price per ad rose 9% in full-year 2025 alone [37]. And the machine deciding who gets seen is explicitly a black box: Meta's Andromeda retrieval engine selects from tens of millions of ad candidates [41], and Zuckerberg's stated end state is that an advertiser connects a bank account, provides no creative and no targeting, and "read[s] the results that we spit out" [42][43].

What a brand can still own is a persistent identity and a corpus of trustworthy, machine-retrievable answers. A new discipline, Generative Engine Optimization (GEO), has emerged to make content citable by models [14], a new KPI, "AI share of voice," measures how often a brand appears in model answers [34], and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets a brand ground a model's answer in its own verified data at query time [25]. But the same data reveals a hard nuance: today most AI citations come from third-party pages, not owned domains [20], and models carry a strong bias toward incumbent brands [28]. The strategic takeaway for FDI is that owning a structured, verifiable corpus and a persistent identity is necessary but must be paired with off-site validation to overcome incumbency bias. This is exactly the pattern the "Pile" build demonstrates: turning a private folder into something a machine can retrieve and cite.

## Background

For three decades, discovery was mediated by a ranked list of blue links. A brand competed for a position, earned a click, and owned the destination. Search Engine Optimization was the practice of winning that position, and paid search was the practice of buying it. The unit of value was the click, and the brand's own website was the endpoint of the journey.

Generative AI has inserted a new layer above the link. Instead of returning a list of sources, an answer engine (Google's AI Overviews, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude) synthesizes an answer and cites a small handful of sources, if any. Google's AI Overviews reach billions of users, and ChatGPT serves on the order of 800–900 million weekly users [4][26]. The endpoint of the journey is increasingly the answer itself, not the brand's site. This changes the fundamental physics of marketing: reach is not something you negotiate with an aggregator, and the click you used to own is now frequently absorbed at the answer layer.

The "Aggregated" thesis of FDI's Growth Cartography series is that this is a structural market shift, not a temporary feature. When discovery moves behind a model, the model becomes an aggregator that decides whether a brand exists in the consideration set. The only durable response is to own the two assets the aggregator cannot rent to you: a persistent, coherent identity, and a corpus of trustworthy answers that any machine can retrieve, verify, and cite. This report assembles the triangulated evidence base for that argument, drawn from academic research (KDD, EMNLP, arXiv), primary consumer research (Pew, Bain, BCG), analytics data (Chartbeat, Similarweb, Semrush, Ahrefs), and the litigation and theory that frame the risk.

## Key Findings

### Finding 1: AI-mediated discovery is measurably displacing the search box

The behavioral shift from searching to asking is documented across multiple independent, high-quality sources. Pew Research Center, analyzing the actual browsing data of 900 U.S. adults across 68,879 Google searches in March 2025, found that 58% of respondents encountered at least one AI-generated summary that month, that users were less likely to click any link when a summary appeared, and, most strikingly, that only about 1% clicked on a source link cited within the AI Overview itself [1]. This is a Tier 1 finding based on observed behavior rather than self-report, which makes it the strongest single anchor for the thesis.

Bain & Company corroborates the magnitude from the demand side: roughly 80% of search users now rely on AI-written summaries for at least 40% of their searches, about 60% of searches end without the user progressing to another website, and Bain estimates this is reducing organic web traffic by 15%–25% [2][3]. Bain also found that about 68% of large-language-model users rely on these platforms to research, gather, and summarize information, and 42% ask for shopping recommendations [2]. On the consumer-commerce side, BCG's January 2026 survey found that generative-AI use in shopping grew 35% between February and November 2025, with shoppers describing the AI's input as "decisive" and confidence-increasing [27].

The substitution is real but should not be overstated, and the honest version of the thesis is stronger for acknowledging the nuance. Orbit Media's March 2026 survey of 1,110 U.S. adults found that more than half now start a search by opening an AI app, but the same study stressed that this share is not rising and that Google usage has not declined, describing the moment as "AI-first enthusiasm moderating into more selective use" [7]. Semrush's study of 10 million keywords found that AI Overviews appeared on 6.5% of queries in January 2025, peaked near 25% in July, then fell back below 16% by November [4], evidence that the rollout is volatile rather than monotonic. The direction of travel is unambiguous; the pace is uneven.

### Finding 2: Referral traffic is collapsing for publishers, and the disintermediation is already in court

The clearest proof that discovery has moved behind the aggregator is the measurable erosion of referral traffic. Chartbeat data published in the Reuters Institute's Journalism and Technology Trends and Predictions 2026 report found that global publisher traffic from Google fell roughly a third (about 33%) in the year to November 2025, with U.S. organic Google referrals down 38% year over year [8]. Digital Content Next's survey of premium publishers found a smaller but consistent median decline of about 10% over an eight-week window, with declines outnumbering gains two-to-one [9]; the gap between the two figures reflects different samples and windows rather than a contradiction, and both point the same direction.

Meanwhile, the absolute volume of AI referral traffic, while growing explosively in percentage terms, remains a rounding error as a replacement. SE Ranking found that traffic from AI search engines grew 16x from 2024 to 2026 but still accounts for only about 0.32% of all website traffic [6]. Digiday, citing Similarweb, found ChatGPT referrals grew 52% year over year while Gemini referrals grew 388%, yet both remain a very small share of publishers' overall traffic [5]. In other words, the click the aggregator absorbs is not being handed back through the citation link. This is the core mechanism of the "Aggregated" thesis: the aggregator keeps the demand relationship and returns very little of it.

The disintermediation risk has moved from analysis to litigation. Chegg sued Google in February 2025, stating it "depends on referrals from Google's monopoly search engine for a large portion of the revenue" and alleging AI summaries hurt its traffic; its stock fell to near $1 and market cap below $200M [10]. In September 2025, Penske Media (Rolling Stone, Variety, Billboard) became the first major U.S. news publisher to sue Google over AI Overviews, alleging a "coercive bargain" in which publishers must allow their content into AI summaries or accept worse search rankings [11][12]. These suits are the strongest possible evidence that dependence on an aggregator for discovery is a strategic vulnerability, not a convenience.

### Finding 3: Generative Engine Optimization is a real, academically grounded discipline for being retrievable and citable

If discovery now happens through model answers, the question becomes what makes a source retrievable and citable by a model. This is the domain of Generative Engine Optimization, and it rests on a peer-reviewed foundation. The original paper, "GEO: Generative Engine Optimization" (arXiv:2311.09735), was authored by researchers from Princeton, Georgia Tech, the Allen Institute for AI, and IIT Delhi and accepted to KDD 2024; it demonstrated that GEO methods, such as citing sources, adding statistics, and including quotations, can boost a source's visibility in generative-engine responses by up to 40%, and it introduced the GEO-bench benchmark [14]. The effect varied by domain, indicating that optimization must be domain-specific. This "40%" figure should be framed as a controlled 2024 benchmark result rather than a live production guarantee, but it establishes that machine-citability is an engineerable property, not luck.

Empirical studies of live engines reveal how concentrated and volatile citation is. Semrush's three-month study of roughly 150,000 citations found Reddit the single most-cited domain at 40.1%, followed by Wikipedia (26.3%), YouTube (23.5%), Google (23.3%), and Yelp (21.0%) [15], a pattern independently visualized by Visual Capitalist from the same dataset [19]. 5WPR's AI Platform Citation Source Index 2026, synthesizing over 680 million citations from six studies, found the top 15 domains capture about 68% of consolidated AI citation share and that platforms cite very differently: ChatGPT concentrates on Wikipedia, Reddit, and business press; Perplexity rewards primary sources and named authorities; Claude leans toward legacy journalism [16]. Citation share is also unstable over weeks: both Semrush and 5WPR documented Reddit's citation share dropping sharply in September 2025, which Semrush tied to Google removing its num=100 search parameter [15][16].

Crucially, ranking and off-site reputation still matter inside the answer layer. Ahrefs found that 38% of pages cited in Google AI Overviews also rank in the top 10 organic results [17], and its study of 75,000 brands reported that web mentions correlated with AI visibility far more strongly than backlinks (roughly 0.66 versus 0.22) [18]. The practical implication is that being retrievable is a combination of on-page structure (GEO) and off-page reputation (mentions), not one or the other.

### Finding 4: "Answer share" is the new visibility KPI, and models carry a strong incumbency bias

As clicks and rankings lose meaning, a new metric has emerged: AI share of voice, defined as how often a brand is cited or mentioned in AI-generated answers relative to competitors. An entire tooling category now exists to measure it, including HubSpot's AEO Grader and independent trackers [34], which is itself evidence that marketers are treating answer presence as a budgeted KPI. AirOps's 2026 State of AI Search report quantifies why the metric matters and how hard it is to hold: only 30% of brands stay visible from one answer to the next, and just 20% remain present across five consecutive runs, though brands earning both a mention and a citation are 40% more likely to reappear [20]. Visibility inside the answer layer is not a position you win once; it is a distribution you must continually defend.

The most consequential finding for brand strategy is that models are not neutral arbiters. A June 2026 arXiv study, "Incumbent Advantage," tested GPT-4o-mini, Claude Sonnet, and Gemini 3 Flash and found that when competing products had identical specifications, well-known incumbent brands were recommended 100% of the time, though this dominance disappeared once a competitor held even a small (roughly 0.1-star) rating advantage [28]. The same paper framed GEO as a competitive "social dilemma," where universal adoption collapses the individual payoff and non-participating brands receive zero recommendations [28]. A peer-reviewed EMNLP 2025 study, "Bias Beware," found cognitive biases such as social proof "deeply embedded" in state-of-the-art LLMs, producing "highly unpredictable" product recommendations [29]. Harvard Business Review similarly reported that LLMs asked for strategic advice tend to converge on homogeneous "trendslop" [30]. Together these establish that the answer layer favors established, well-attested identities, which is precisely why a persistent, well-documented brand identity is a defensible asset.

### Finding 5: Owned corpora, RAG, and structured content are the mechanism, with a third-party paradox to solve

The FDI thesis rests on owning a corpus of trustworthy, machine-retrievable answers. The technical mechanism for grounding a model in that corpus is retrieval-augmented generation, which retrieves verified, proprietary data at generation time rather than relying on static training data [25]. The RAG market reflects the momentum: one market-research estimate put it at $2.33B in 2025, growing to $3.33B in 2026 and projected toward $81.5B by 2035 at a ~43% CAGR [24] (a single-firm estimate, directionally useful rather than consensus). The strategic logic is clean: a brand that structures its own knowledge as a retrievable corpus can be the grounded source a model cites, rather than hoping to be recalled from parametric memory.

The tooling for machine-readability is maturing but immature. The llms.txt convention, proposed in September 2024 by Jeremy Howard, offers a curated markdown map of a site's high-value content on the premise that context windows are too small to ingest whole websites and that stripping HTML is imprecise [21]. But adoption by the major model providers remains unconfirmed; as Ahrefs bluntly summarized, no major provider currently commits to crawling it [22][23]. Structured data (schema.org) is more established, with vendor analyses claiming meaningful citation lifts, though those specific multipliers rest on Tier 3 sources and should be treated as directional rather than proven [32]. AirOps found that pages not updated quarterly are 3x more likely to lose citations and that rich schema with sequential headings correlated with 2.8x higher citation rates [20], reinforcing that freshness and structure are levers a brand controls.

The paradox that FDI's writer must confront head-on is this: AirOps found that roughly 85% of brand mentions in AI answers originate from third-party pages rather than owned domains, and about 48% of citations come from community platforms like Reddit and YouTube [20]. This complicates a naive "just own your corpus" message. The honest, stronger framing is that an owned corpus is necessary infrastructure, the durable, verifiable ground truth a model can retrieve, but it must be paired with earned off-site validation (mentions, community presence, primary-source authority) to actually win citations against incumbency bias. This is the sophisticated version of the "Pile" argument: the private folder becomes machine-retrievable ground truth, and the brand's job is then to make that ground truth the most-referenced, most-verifiable node in a wider web of signals.

### Finding 6: Three platforms now absorb over three-fifths of the world's digital ad spending, and the concentration is still increasing

The pre-AI half of the aggregated market is defined by extreme and still-rising concentration. eMarketer forecasts that Meta will generate $243.46 billion in net worldwide ad revenue in 2026 (26.8% of global digital ad spending), surpassing Google ($239.54 billion, 26.4%) for the first time; Amazon takes third at roughly 9% ($82.07 billion), and the three together account for 62.3% of global digital ad spending, a combined share eMarketer expects to keep rising through 2028 [35][36]. The consolidation logic named by eMarketer's analysts is the same one that powers the AI answer layer: first-party data, reach, and AI integration [36]. Meta's actuals anchor the forecast: full-year 2025 advertising revenue was $196.175 billion, up 22% year over year [37].

The forecast figures [35][36] are projections and should be labeled as such in the guide, but the actuals-based trendline is unambiguous, and eMarketer's own framing ("the question is not whether they should spend money on Meta's apps, the question is how much") captures how little optionality most advertisers believe they have [36].

### Finding 7: Paid reach compounds in price, and the platform decides the price

Meta's reported numbers document the compounding cost of rented reach: average price per ad rose 9% year over year in full-year 2025 (6% in Q4), while ad impressions delivered rose 12% (18% in Q4) [37]. The price of access rises even as the platform manufactures more inventory. Independent benchmark data adds useful nuance rather than a single "CPM inflation" story: Tinuiti's Q3 2025 benchmark (about $4B in managed media) found Meta ad spending up 14% year over year on 16% impression growth with CPM down 2%, Facebook CPM down 6% on 15% more impressions (Reels inventory diluting price), Google search spend up 10% with CPC down 1% (Amazon's temporary exit from shopping auctions), and TikTok spend down 4% with CPM down 22% amid its U.S. uncertainty [38].

The honest version of this finding is therefore not "prices only go up." It is that price, volume, and placement are all set unilaterally by the platform, quarter by quarter, for its own optimization goals. An advertiser's costs can fall because a rival left an auction or a new surface diluted inventory, and rise again just as suddenly. The variable that never changes hands is control.

### Finding 8: Organic reach has collapsed to a rounding error, and follower counts barely matter

Organic distribution inside the major platforms is no longer a plannable channel. Socialinsider's 2025 benchmark data puts average organic reach per post at about 1.65% of page followers on Facebook and 3.50% on Instagram, both declining year over year [39]. On TikTok the decoupling of audience from distribution is structural: an analysis of 31,059 posts with traffic-source data found 85.1% of views arrive via the algorithmic For You page, with the Following feed delivering roughly 0.3%, search about 1.1%, and profile visits about 2% [40]. Following an account is a preference signal to the recommender, not a subscription: even a creator's own followers mostly see their content only if the For You algorithm re-selects it [40].

The strategic meaning is stark: the "audience" a brand builds inside an aggregator is not an owned asset. It is a lookup key into someone else's recommender system. This is the platform-half mirror of Finding 4's answer-share volatility, and it is the empirical basis for the series' core distinction between rented reach and owned assets.

### Finding 9: The ad machine is an explicit black box, and the platforms say so themselves

The opacity of platform distribution is not an accusation; it is the product roadmap. Meta's own engineering blog describes Andromeda, the retrieval engine behind Advantage+ automation, as selecting from "tens of millions of ad candidates" down to a few thousand per impression opportunity using deep neural networks co-designed with NVIDIA and MTIA hardware [41]. Zuckerberg described the end state in a May 2025 Stratechery interview: "you're a business, you come to us, you tell us what your objective is, you connect to your bank account, you don't need any creative, you don't need any targeting demographic, you don't need any measurement, except to be able to read the results that we spit out" [42][43]. Targeting, creative, and measurement, the three functions marketing departments were built around, are all absorbed into the aggregator.

This is the deepest continuity between the two halves of the aggregated market: today's ad platforms and tomorrow's answer engines are the same structural machine at different maturities. Both interpose a model between the brand and the audience, both absorb the levers marketers used to hold, and both leave exactly one input in the brand's hands: the assets it shows up with, its identity, its story, its corpus, its evidence. That residual input is the entire strategic surface the FDI thesis addresses.

## Analysis

Read together, the findings describe a single structural transition and a single defensible response. The transition is the insertion of an aggregating answer layer between brands and customers. Every focus area confirms the same mechanism from a different angle: consumers are asking models (Finding 1), the models absorb the click and starve the destination (Finding 2), the models cite a tiny, concentrated, volatile set of sources (Finding 3), they measure and reward established identity through an emergent "answer share" while carrying incumbency bias (Finding 4), and the only content that can be reliably surfaced is structured, fresh, verifiable, and cross-validated (Finding 5). This is Aggregation Theory extended: the aggregator owns demand, and suppliers, brands and publishers alike, are commoditized unless they own something the aggregator cannot substitute away [13].

The most important cross-cutting insight is that the two assets the aggregator cannot rent to you are exactly the two the answer layer rewards. First, persistent identity: because models favor well-attested incumbents [28][29] and reward brands that earn both mentions and citations [20], a coherent, recognizable, heavily-referenced identity is not a branding nicety but a retrieval advantage. Second, an owned, machine-retrievable corpus: because models ground answers in retrievable, verifiable, fresh, structured content [20][25], a brand that turns its knowledge into a citable corpus makes itself the source rather than the summarized. FDI's "identity as the ultimate infrastructure" argument from the first Field Guide is not merely still true in the aggregated market; it is more true, because the aggregator has made identity and owned corpus the only inputs a brand still controls.

The counter-evidence sharpens rather than undermines the thesis. The plateau in AI-first behavior [7], the pullback in AI Overview coverage [4], and the tiny absolute share of AI referral traffic [6] all warn against declaring search dead. But the thesis was never "search is dead"; it is "discovery is being intermediated, and the intermediary does not return the relationship." A 33% collapse in publisher referrals [8] and two federal lawsuits [10][11] are sufficient to establish that dependence on rented reach is now a quantifiable risk, regardless of how fast the last mile of adoption moves. Likewise, the third-party citation paradox [20] does not refute the owned-corpus thesis; it disciplines it, requiring that owned infrastructure be paired with earned validation.

Finally, the volatility documented everywhere, week-scale swings in citation share [15][16], 30%-of-brands single-turn persistence [20], unpredictable bias-driven recommendations [29], is itself an argument for owned infrastructure over rented tactics. When the surface layer is this unstable, the only sane strategy is to invest in the durable substrate (identity, corpus, verifiable interfaces) rather than chasing an optimization that decays in six weeks.

## Limitations

Several important caveats bound these findings. First, no major model provider (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Perplexity) publishes its citation-selection or retrieval-weighting algorithms, so every "how models choose sources" claim is inferred from third-party observational studies rather than primary documentation. Second, key quantitative claims vary by methodology and should not be treated as interchangeable: zero-click and traffic-decline figures differ between clickstream panels (Chartbeat, Similarweb), keyword panels (Semrush), and surveys (Bain, Orbit), and the widely-repeated Gartner "25% search decline by 2026" prediction traces to a 2024 statement and was not verified against a primary release. Third, some striking figures rest on single or non-peer-reviewed sources: the "Incumbent Advantage" 100%-recommendation result is a June 2026 preprint [28], and specific schema/citation-lift multipliers come from Tier 3 vendor content [32]. Fourth, there is no longitudinal, brand-owned-corpus case study with measured citation outcomes, so the causal link between "own a corpus" and "win citations" is supported directionally (via mention-correlation and GEO-bench experiments) rather than proven. Finally, several platform user-count figures surfaced only through search-answer aggregation and should be confirmed against primary statements before publication.

## Recommendations

For the writer of the "Aggregated" Field Guide, the research supports a confident, defensible argument built on the following moves.

**Lead with the behavioral proof, then name the mechanism.** Open with the Tier 1 findings, Pew's 1%-click-through [1] and Bain's 60%-zero-click / 15–25% traffic loss [2][3], because they are observed, not self-reported. Then name the mechanism as Aggregation Theory one layer up [13]: the answer engine is an aggregator that owns demand and commoditizes suppliers. This frames the whole guide.

**Use the lawsuits as the emotional and strategic hook.** Chegg [10] and Penske's "coercive bargain" [11][12], set against the 33% publisher-traffic collapse [8], make the risk of rented reach concrete and urgent in a way statistics alone cannot.

**Position owned identity and an owned corpus as the only defensible response, and be honest about the third-party paradox.** Do not claim that simply owning a corpus wins citations. The stronger, research-backed argument is that an owned, structured, fresh, verifiable corpus [20][25] is necessary infrastructure that must be paired with earned off-site validation to beat incumbency bias [28][29]. This nuance will make the guide credible to sophisticated CMOs who already know that Reddit outranks their own site in AI answers [15][20].

**Make the Pile build the concrete proof.** Pile turns a private folder into a structured, machine-retrievable, citable corpus, which is precisely the owned infrastructure the aggregated market rewards [14][20][25]. Present it as the working answer to "how do I become the source a model cites instead of the click a model absorbs?" Emphasize GEO as an engineerable property [14], AI share of voice as the KPI it lets you defend [20][34], and verifiability as the moat against the answer layer's documented volatility and bias.

**Tie back to the series thesis.** The first Field Guide argued identity is the ultimate infrastructure. Aggregated should show that the aggregator has made this literally true: identity and an owned corpus are now the only marketing inputs a brand still controls, and every quarter spent buying commoditized reach is a quarter not spent building the assets that survive the aggregator.

**Open the guide with the platform half, because it is the story the reader already believes.** Every marketer has lived the organic reach collapse (1.65% on Facebook, 3.50% on Instagram [39]), the follower-irrelevance of TikTok's For You page (85.1% of views, 0.3% from Following [40]), and the compounding price of paid access (average price per ad +9% in 2025 [37]) inside a market where three companies take 62.3% of digital ad spending [35][36]. Zuckerberg's "connect to your bank account" quote [42][43] and the Andromeda black box [41] then set up the pivot: the AI answer layer is not a new problem, it is the same machine arriving at the discovery layer, before a single dollar is spent. That pivot makes the AI-half evidence (Pew, Bain, the lawsuits) land as confirmation rather than speculation, and it makes the owned-assets conclusion feel inevitable rather than ideological.
