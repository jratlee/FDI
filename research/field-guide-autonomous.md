# Autonomous: Why Your Marketing System Has to Be Something Another Agent Can Safely Call

**Research Date:** July 5, 2026
**Depth:** Standard (5 parallel focus areas, gap-checked)
**Sources Consulted:** 30
**Prepared for:** False Dawn Industries — Growth Cartography series, Field Guide 004 ("Autonomous")

## Executive Summary

The premise of the "Autonomous" Field Guide is no longer a forecast. AI agents are already transacting on customers' behalf, the money is real, and the plumbing is standardizing in the open. In September 2025 two rival protocol stacks launched within two weeks of each other: OpenAI and Stripe published the Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP) on September 29 and simultaneously turned on Instant Checkout inside ChatGPT, where more than 700 million weekly users can now buy from Etsy sellers and, rolling out, over a million Shopify merchants without leaving the chat [1][2][3]. Google announced the Agent Payments Protocol (AP2) on September 16 with more than sixty payments and technology partners, including Mastercard, American Express, PayPal, Coinbase, and Worldpay [4][5]. The demand side is already there: Morgan Stanley reported in December 2025 that roughly 23% of Americans had made a purchase using AI in the prior month and projected agentic shoppers will capture 10% to 20% of US e-commerce, $190 billion to $385 billion, by 2030 [7]; McKinsey put the global figure at $3 trillion to $5 trillion of orchestrated commerce by 2030 [8]; and Adobe, measuring more than a trillion visits to US retail sites, found AI-referred traffic up 393% year over year in Q1 2026 and up 693% over the 2025 holiday season, with 39% of consumers saying they had already used AI to shop [10].

The thesis is that when an agent, not a human, is the buyer and the researcher, a marketing system has to be something that agent can *call*: a gated, citable, machine-legible interface. The interface layer is consolidating fast. The Model Context Protocol (MCP), the de facto open standard for exposing a system as an agent-callable tool, was donated by Anthropic to the Linux Foundation's new Agentic AI Foundation on December 9, 2025, co-founded with Block and OpenAI, and reported 97 million-plus monthly SDK downloads and more than 10,000 active public servers, with client support across ChatGPT, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Cursor, and VS Code [11][12][13][15]. A complementary standard, Google's Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol, went to the Linux Foundation and reached a stable 1.0, letting agents from different vendors discover and coordinate with each other; AP2 is explicitly designed to run as an extension of both A2A and MCP [4][17][19][20].

The other half of the thesis, the trust boundaries, is where the authoritative evidence is strongest and the urgency clearest. OWASP ranks prompt injection as the number one risk to LLM applications and, in December 2025, published a dedicated Top 10 for Agentic Applications reviewed by NIST, the European Commission, and the Alan Turing Institute [21][22]. The core failure mode is precisely "data becoming a command": models cannot reliably tell trusted instructions from untrusted retrieved content, the problem Simon Willison names the "lethal trifecta" and academics formalize as indirect prompt injection [26][28], and which Google DeepMind and ETH Zurich argue must be solved by design rather than by asking the model to behave [27]. This stopped being theoretical in November 2025, when Anthropic disclosed the first documented large-scale cyberattack executed largely by an AI agent [25]. The strategic conclusion for brands is that the same properties that make an owned system trustworthy to a human, being gated, citable, and machine-legible, are exactly what make it safely callable by an agent. FDI's builds are early instances: Talk to NYC exposes a citable knowledge graph through an MCP server, and Pile puts a gated, verifiable agent API in front of paid answers.

## Background

For thirty years, marketing optimized for a human at a screen: an eyeball to capture, a click to earn, a cart to nudge. The autonomous shift breaks that assumption at the interface. Increasingly the entity doing the searching, comparing, and buying is software acting under delegated authority: it receives a goal in natural language, plans steps, queries merchants, evaluates options against the user's constraints, authorizes payment within preset limits, and completes an order [1][8]. Google states the problem plainly: "today's payment systems generally assume a human is directly clicking 'buy' on a trusted surface," and autonomous agents "break this fundamental assumption" [4]. When the buyer is a program, a brand's website, ad, and funnel are no longer the interface; the interface is whatever structured surface the agent can read, trust, and invoke.

Three technical developments make this concrete and urgent at once. First, agentic *commerce* protocols (ACP, AP2) now define how an agent completes a purchase and proves the user authorized it [1][4]. Second, an agent-callable *tool* standard (MCP) defines how any system exposes its data and functions to an agent as callable tools, resources, and prompts [14]. Third, an agent-to-agent *coordination* standard (A2A) defines how agents from different vendors discover and delegate to one another [17][20]. Layered together, these describe an economy in which your customer's agent calls other agents and tools to get something done, and your marketing system is worth exactly as much as it is callable, trustworthy, and legible to that stack. This report assembles the evidence across five focus areas: agents transacting on users' behalf; MCP as the agent-callable interface; agent-to-agent interoperability; the security and trust-boundary model for agent-facing systems; and what it means for a brand to become a gated, citable, machine-legible interface. Sources span protocol specifications and standards bodies (OpenAI/Stripe, Google, the Linux Foundation, OWASP, NIST, MITRE, W3C), primary vendor disclosures (Anthropic), analyst and bank research (Morgan Stanley, McKinsey, Bain), primary measurement (Adobe), and peer-reviewed academic work (KDD 2024, arXiv).

## Key Findings

### Finding 1: Agents are already transacting on customers' behalf, with real money, open protocols, and measurable adoption

The single most important fact for the "Autonomous" thesis is that agentic commerce moved from concept to production in the second half of 2025, and it did so through open standards rather than a single walled garden. On September 29, 2025, Stripe and OpenAI published the Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP) under Apache 2.0 and switched on Instant Checkout in ChatGPT, letting US users buy from US Etsy sellers directly in chat, with "over a million Shopify merchants, such as Glossier, Vuori, Spanx, and SKIMS," described as coming soon [1][2]. Stripe frames ACP as "a blueprint for how businesses can make their checkouts agent-ready so that customers using AI agents, such as ChatGPT, can buy products directly from where they're discovering them" [1]. Salesforce announced support for ACP in collaboration with Stripe within weeks, signaling that the standard is being wired into enterprise commerce platforms rather than staying an OpenAI-only feature [3]. The scale of the surface is the point: OpenAI states more than 700 million people use ChatGPT weekly, which turns a chat product into one of the largest points of sale on the internet the moment checkout is embedded [1].

Two weeks earlier, on September 16, 2025, Google announced the Agent Payments Protocol (AP2), "an open protocol developed with leading payments and technology companies to securely initiate and transact agent-led payments across platforms," in collaboration with more than sixty organizations including Mastercard, American Express, PayPal, Coinbase, Adyen, Worldpay, Intuit, and Salesforce [4]. Critically, AP2 is designed to interoperate, not compete, with the tool and agent standards: Google says it "can be used as an extension of the Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol and Model Context Protocol (MCP)" [4]. The existence of two credible, backer-heavy open protocols launched almost simultaneously is itself the finding: the market is standardizing agent checkout now, and the participants are the incumbents of payments and commerce, not fringe startups, a shift independent analysts read as a genuine "new chapter in agentic commerce" [1][4][3][30].

The demand behind the protocols is documented by independent, non-vendor sources. Morgan Stanley reported on December 8, 2025 that "roughly 23% of Americans made purchases using AI in the past month" and projected that agentic shoppers "could represent $190 billion to $385 billion in U.S. e-commerce spending by 2030, capturing 10% to 20% of market share," with groceries and consumer packaged goods the likely largest growth driver [7]. McKinsey's QuantumBlack, in October 2025, estimated that agentic commerce could orchestrate up to $1 trillion in US B2C retail revenue and $3 trillion to $5 trillion globally by 2030, noting these figures cover goods only and exclude services and B2B [8]. Bain & Company published a parallel 2030 US-retail forecast in the same window [9]. Adobe, which bases its numbers on more than a trillion visits to US retail sites plus a survey of over 5,000 US consumers, is the strongest primary measurement: AI-referred traffic to US retail rose 393% year over year in Q1 2026 (and 693% over the November–December 2025 holiday), 39% of consumers reported having used AI to shop with 85% of them saying it improved the experience, and AI traffic converts to purchase better than non-AI channels [10]. The forecasts diverge by scope and method (Morgan Stanley scopes US e-commerce, McKinsey scopes global orchestrated revenue), so they should be cited as a range with attribution rather than a single number, but every independent source points the same direction and near-term [7][8][9][10].

### Finding 2: The interface is standardizing on MCP, the open protocol for exposing a system as an agent-callable tool

If agents are the new buyers, the question becomes how any system makes itself callable by them, and the answer that has won is the Model Context Protocol. Anthropic open-sourced MCP on November 25, 2024 as "an open standard that enables developers to build secure, two-way connections between their data sources and AI-powered tools," with servers that expose three primitives over JSON-RPC 2.0: Resources (context and data), Prompts (templated workflows), and Tools (functions the model can execute) [11][14]. This is exactly the "expose your system as an agent-callable tool" pattern the Autonomous thesis calls for; Anthropic's own engineering team now describes MCP as "the de-facto standard for connecting agents to tools and data" [15]. The decisive evidence that MCP is a genuinely open standard and not a proprietary hook is governance: on December 9, 2025 Anthropic donated MCP to the Agentic AI Foundation, a directed fund under the Linux Foundation co-founded with Block and OpenAI, with platinum members including AWS, Bloomberg, Cloudflare, Google, and Microsoft [12][13]. For FDI's copy this matters, because it lets the site describe MCP as an open, foundation-governed standard without claiming any single vendor's endorsement.

Adoption is broad and cross-vendor, and the numbers that survive primary-source scrutiny are large. Anthropic's donation announcement reports "over 97 million monthly SDK downloads" across Python and TypeScript and "more than 10,000 active public MCP servers, covering everything from developer tools to Fortune 500 deployments," backed by an official community registry [12]. Client support is not confined to one model family: MCP "has been adopted by ChatGPT, Cursor, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Visual Studio Code, and other popular AI products," and OpenAI's adoption in particular was independently confirmed by TechCrunch on March 26, 2025 ("OpenAI adopts rival Anthropic's standard for connecting AI models to data") [12][16]. A single launch figure that circulates in AI-generated summaries, "110 million monthly downloads," could not be traced to a primary source and should not be used; the defensible, primary-sourced figures are 97 million-plus monthly downloads and 10,000-plus servers as of December 2025 [12].

The specification itself validates the trust-boundary half of the FDI thesis. The MCP spec states bluntly that "Tools represent arbitrary code execution and must be treated with appropriate caution," requires host approval of servers, and has evolved to harden authorization: the March 2025 revision added OAuth 2.1 and a streamable HTTP transport, June 2025 added structured tool outputs and an OAuth resource-server model, and the November 2025 revision added server identity and asynchronous operations [14]. Tool poisoning, where a malicious or altered tool description manipulates the calling model, is a tracked, real vulnerability class rather than a hypothetical [14]. In other words, the standard the ecosystem chose already assumes the caller must not trust the callable surface blindly, which is the same principle FDI applies from the server side.

### Finding 3: Agents coordinate with each other, and the protocol stack for that is converging in the open

Autonomy is not one agent calling one tool; it is agents calling other agents, and that interoperability layer standardized on Google's Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol. Google announced A2A at Cloud Next on April 9, 2025 with more than fifty founding partners, framing it as "a common language for agents" so that agents "built on diverse frameworks by different companies running on separate servers" can "communicate and collaborate effectively, as agents, not just as tools" [17]. Like MCP, A2A went to a neutral home: the Linux Foundation launched the A2A Protocol project, and by its one-year mark on April 9, 2026 the foundation reported more than 150 supporting organizations, deep integration across Google, Microsoft, and AWS, active enterprise production use across supply chain, financial services, insurance, and IT operations, and a stable 1.0 specification with a public RFC process [18][19][20]. A2A's distinguishing mechanic is dynamic discovery through "Agent Cards," which let one agent find another's identity, capabilities, and endpoints at runtime rather than being wired together in advance [17][20].

The relationship between the standards is complementary, not competitive, and understanding it sharpens the FDI argument. MCP is the vertical connection, an agent reaching down to tools and data; A2A is the horizontal connection, agents reaching across to each other; and AP2 is the payment layer that rides on both [4][17]. Google explicitly positions AP2 as "an extension of the Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol and Model Context Protocol (MCP)" [4], and A2A's founding partners and cloud integrations confirm the horizontal layer is real production infrastructure rather than a spec on paper [19]. For a brand, the implication is that a customer's agent will not just read your content; it may delegate a purchase to a shopping agent that in turn calls your MCP server and settles through AP2 or ACP. Being callable at each layer, discoverable (an agent-card-style presence), invocable (an MCP tool), and transactable (an ACP/AP2 endpoint), is the new distribution. It is worth noting for balance that A2A adoption figures come substantially from the Linux Foundation and Google, its principal backers; the "more than 150 organizations" and production-use claims are credible and multi-industry but should be attributed to the project rather than treated as neutral third-party audit [19].

### Finding 4: The security model is the product: agent-facing systems must treat every input as hostile and never let data become a command

The most authoritative and most urgent evidence supports the trust-boundary discipline at the heart of the Autonomous thesis. OWASP ranks prompt injection as the number one risk on its Top 10 for LLM Applications (LLM01:2025), defining it as occurring "when user prompts alter the LLM's behavior or output in unintended ways," and stressing that injections "do not need to be human-visible/readable, as long as the content is parsed by the model" and that RAG and fine-tuning "do not fully mitigate" the problem [21]. OWASP names the exact mechanism the FDI thesis warns about: indirect prompt injection, which occurs "when an LLM accepts input from external sources, such as websites or files," whose content carries hidden instructions [21]. This is "data becoming a command" stated by the field's leading application-security body. On December 9, 2025 OWASP extended this into a dedicated Top 10 for Agentic Applications, built with input from more than a hundred experts and reviewed by an expert board including NIST, the European Commission, and the Alan Turing Institute, highlighting agent behavior/goal hijacking, tool misuse and exploitation, and identity and privilege abuse as the defining agentic threats [22]. The government-standards layer corroborates it: NIST's AI 100-2e2025, "Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations" (March 2025), establishes shared terminology for these attacks, and MITRE ATLAS provides an ATT&CK-style knowledge base of real-world AI adversary techniques including prompt injection [23][24].

The reason this is an architectural problem rather than a tuning problem is well established. Simon Willison, who coined the term "prompt injection," describes the "lethal trifecta": an agent becomes exploitable for data theft the moment it combines access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and the ability to communicate externally, because "LLMs are unable to reliably distinguish the importance of instructions based on where they came from" and "will happily follow any instructions that make it to the model" [26]. The seminal academic demonstration, Greshake et al.'s "Not what you've signed up for" (2023), first showed that retrieved external content can hijack LLM-integrated applications; it predates the 18-month freshness window and should be cited as foundational rather than current [28]. The design-level fix is now published: Google DeepMind and ETH Zurich's CaMeL paper, "Defeating Prompt Injections by Design" (March 2025), diagnoses "the original sin" as concatenating trusted user prompts and untrusted external text into the same token stream, and argues the solution is to separate control flow from data at the system level rather than relying on the model to self-distinguish, which is the engineering expression of "never let data become a command" [27]. The classic confused-deputy problem, a privileged program tricked by a less-privileged party into misusing its authority, is increasingly described as the default architecture of tool-using agents, reinforcing the case for least privilege and explicit trust boundaries [24].

This is no longer hypothetical. In November 2025 Anthropic disclosed that in mid-September 2025 it had detected a state-sponsored group that "manipulated our Claude Code tool into attempting infiltration into roughly thirty global targets," calling it "the first documented case of a large-scale cyberattack executed without substantial human intervention" [25]. The incident is a concrete example of tool misuse and excessive agency, the exact categories OWASP's agentic list warns about, and although the framing comes from the vendor whose tool was abused, the direction is clear: as agents gain the ability to act, the blast radius of a successful injection grows from a bad answer to a real action [25][22]. During this research, several eye-catching breach statistics (attributed in AI-generated summaries to CrowdStrike, Adversa, and Obsidian) and two arXiv identifiers could not be traced to primary sources and were deliberately excluded; the verified frameworks, research, and the Anthropic incident are more than enough to carry the argument. The takeaway for FDI is that the security posture is not a caveat bolted onto an agent-facing marketing system; it is the feature that makes the system trustworthy enough to be worth calling.

### Finding 5: The brand becomes a gated, citable, machine-legible interface, and legibility is now measurable and scarce

When agents mediate discovery and purchase, the winning brand asset is not a persuasive page but a machine-readable, citable, callable surface, and there is now both an academic foundation and hard measurement behind this. The academic origin is Aggarwal, Murahari, and colleagues' "GEO: Generative Engine Optimization," published at KDD 2024, which formalizes generative engines as systems that "generate accurate and personalized responses… by synthesizing information from multiple sources" and identifies the new problem that content creators "have little to no control over when and how their content is displayed," proposing optimization methods to improve a source's visibility inside generated answers [29]. Generative Engine Optimization and its sibling Answer Engine Optimization have since become recognized 2026 marketing practices for making content citable inside model answers, which is the discovery half of an agent-facing brand [29].

The scarcity of machine-legibility is quantified by Adobe's April 2026 report, which is the cleanest evidence for the "machine-legible interface" argument. Measuring more than a trillion retail visits, Adobe found that "major portions of U.S. retail websites are not entirely readable by machines, which limits their visibility across AI search results," and warned that "as the internet continues to be reintermediated by AI, there is increased urgency for retailers to revamp their digital properties" [10]. In the same report, AI is described as "quickly becoming the primary interface between consumers and their favorite brands," AI-referred traffic converts better than non-AI channels, and yet many brands are structurally invisible to the very agents doing the buying [10]. This is the marketing consequence of Findings 1 through 4 stated in dollars and traffic: the demand is routing through agents, the interfaces (MCP, A2A, ACP, AP2) exist, and the brands that have not made themselves legible and callable are being left out of answers and carts they cannot see.

The convergence across all five areas is what makes the brand-as-interface claim more than a slogan. ACP's whole purpose is to make a checkout "agent-ready" [1]; AP2 exists so a merchant can *verify* that an agent's request reflects genuine user intent, via cryptographically signed "mandates" built on the W3C Verifiable Credentials standard, which is a gate and a proof, not a vibe [4][6]; MCP is the callable-tool surface; and GEO/AEO plus machine-readability are the citable, legible surface [10][29]. A gated, citable, machine-legible interface is precisely the intersection of these standards. That is the form a brand identity now has to take to survive an agent-mediated market, and it is a direct extension of the first Field Guide's "identity as the ultimate infrastructure": the persistent, recognizable identity must now also be one a machine can find, trust, verify, and call.

## Analysis

Read together, the five findings describe a single, fast-closing shift. Agents are already buying at scale, and the payment and commerce protocols for that are live and open (Finding 1). The way any system makes itself callable by those agents has standardized on MCP, now a foundation-governed open standard with cross-vendor adoption (Finding 2). Agents increasingly coordinate with each other through A2A, so a brand's callable surface will be invoked not only by a user's assistant but by other agents delegating to it (Finding 3). The entire stack is only safe if agent-facing systems treat every retrieved input as hostile, sanitize what they emit, and never let data become a command, a discipline OWASP, NIST, MITRE, leading researchers, and a real November 2025 incident all now insist on (Finding 4). And the marketing consequence is that a brand becomes valuable exactly to the degree it is a gated, citable, machine-legible interface, which Adobe shows most brands are not yet (Finding 5).

The cross-cutting insight is that the two things that make an owned system *trustworthy* and the two things that make it *callable* are the same things. An agent will only rely on a source it can verify (citation) and only invoke a surface it can authenticate and bound (gating), and a machine can only do either if the surface is legible. That is why the security discussion is not a defensive appendix to the commerce story; it is the enabling condition for it. AP2's mandates are a gate and a proof of intent [4][6]; MCP's OAuth and host-approval model are a gate on the tool surface [14]; citation-preserving retrieval is what lets an agent trust an answer enough to act on it. The brand that internalizes this stops thinking of its marketing system as content to be seen and starts building it as an interface to be called: discoverable like an agent card, invocable like an MCP tool, transactable like an ACP or AP2 endpoint, and defended like a system that assumes its inputs are adversarial.

This is exactly the logic FDI's builds already embody, which is what makes the "Autonomous" guide a demonstration rather than a prediction. Talk to NYC exposes a citable legal knowledge graph through an MCP server, so another agent can query it as a tool while every answer keeps its section numbers attached, satisfying the callable-and-citable requirement at once. Pile puts a gated agent API in front of paid, provably-honest answers, so an agent can transact against it with the same integrity a human gets. Skillfoundry's MCP interface makes an owned capability agent-callable by design. Each is an early, working instance of the gated, citable, machine-legible interface the market is now standardizing around, built with the trust boundaries (treat retrieved documents as hostile, sanitize outputs, never let data become a command) that the security literature says are non-negotiable.

## Limitations

This is a fast-moving area, and several caveats apply. Market-size forecasts for agentic commerce vary widely by scope and methodology (Morgan Stanley scopes US e-commerce; McKinsey scopes global orchestrated goods revenue; Bain scopes US retail), so any single dollar figure should be cited as a range with attribution rather than a settled number [7][8][9]. Some vendor adoption and breach statistics that circulate in AI-generated search summaries, including a "110 million monthly MCP downloads" figure and several 2025–2026 prompt-injection incident counts attributed to security vendors, could not be traced to primary documents and were deliberately excluded; only primary-sourced figures were used. A2A's headline adoption numbers originate substantially from the Linux Foundation and Google, its principal backers, and should be read as project-reported rather than independently audited [19]. Two research inputs predate the 18-month freshness window and are used only as foundational: Greshake et al. (2023) on indirect prompt injection [28] and Anthropic's original MCP announcement (November 2024) for definitional facts [11]. Finally, the Anthropic GTG-1002 incident is a primary vendor disclosure about an attack on the vendor's own tool; its facts are credible and corroborated by law-firm client alerts, but the "first of its kind" framing is the vendor's [25]. Empirical, at-scale measurement of how well specific mitigations (output sanitization, least privilege, human-in-the-loop) actually reduce agent risk remains thin; most mitigation guidance is framework-level rather than measured.

## Recommendations

For the writer of the "Autonomous" Field Guide, the evidence supports a confident, demonstrable argument, and these are the moves that will make it land.

Lead with the fact, not the forecast: agents are already transacting, through ACP in ChatGPT and AP2 across sixty-plus payments partners, with roughly a quarter of Americans already buying via AI and AI retail traffic up several hundred percent year over year [1][4][7][10]. This defuses the "someday" objection immediately. Then make the interface argument concrete by naming the stack, MCP for tools, A2A for agent-to-agent coordination, ACP/AP2 for payment, and stressing that all four are open and increasingly foundation-governed, so building to them is building on standards, not betting on one vendor [12][13][19][4]. Anchor the security section in authority: OWASP ranks prompt injection number one and shipped an agentic Top 10 reviewed by NIST and the Alan Turing Institute; the "lethal trifecta" and CaMeL explain why it is an architecture problem; and the November 2025 Anthropic incident shows the stakes are now real actions, not bad answers [21][22][26][27][25]. Keep the three trust-boundary rules verbatim as the guide's memorable spine: treat every retrieved document as hostile input, sanitize everything that goes out, and never let data become a command, and attribute the principle to the field (OWASP indirect injection, CaMeL's control/data separation) so it reads as consensus, not FDI opinion [21][27].

Land the marketing thesis on Adobe's scarcity finding: AI is becoming the primary interface between consumers and brands, yet most retail sites are not machine-readable, so machine-legibility is a present, measurable competitive gap, not a future nicety [10]. Close by tying it to the builds: Talk to NYC (citable graph over an MCP server), Pile (gated, verifiable agent API), and Skillfoundry's MCP are working proof that a marketing system can be something another agent safely calls, and that the gate, the citation, and the machine-legibility are the same asset viewed from three sides. Avoid citing any unverified vendor statistic or the untraceable download figure; the primary-sourced evidence assembled here is more than sufficient and keeps the guide's credibility intact.
