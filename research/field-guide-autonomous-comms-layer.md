## 1. Why This Appendix Exists

The autonomous-market research documents a shift that most PR professionals will initially dismiss as irrelevant to their work: AI agents transacting on behalf of users, payment protocols for machine-to-machine commerce, and security boundaries for agent-callable systems. The instinctive response is that this is a developer story, not a communications story.

That instinct is understandable and wrong. When an AI agent researching a purchase, a topic, or a brand recommendation generates an answer, selects a source, or completes a transaction, the inputs it uses are the brand's machine-readable, citable, structured presence. The communications professional who understands what makes a brand discoverable to an agent is in the same position as the communications professional who understood what made a brand rankable in search a decade ago: ahead of where the measurement follows.

This appendix identifies the PR/comms KPIs that matter in autonomous markets, names five well-known real-world illustrations that a communications professional will recognize, and provides the budget framing that the field guide outline will need.

## 2. Agents as the New "Reporters" and "Editors"

### The reframe

In traditional media relations, a reporter or editor is the human intermediary between a brand's story and its audience. The PR professional's job is to make the brand's story accessible, credible, and relevant to that human intermediary. The reporter then exercises judgment about what to include, how to frame it, and which sources to trust.

In autonomous markets, AI agents are performing a functionally similar role. When a user delegates a research task to an AI agent, the agent queries available sources, evaluates their credibility, synthesizes an answer, and presents it to the user. The user trusts the agent's output the way they would trust a well-briefed assistant or researcher.

The PR implication is direct: the brand needs its information to pass the credibility test that an agent applies, not only the credibility test that a human reporter applies. The agent's credibility test is different: it relies on machine-readable structure, citable sourcing, verifiable provenance, and the brand's presence in trusted third-party references. A beautiful press release that is not structured for machine retrieval fails the agent's test even if it would succeed with a human reporter.

### What "citable by an agent" means in practice

For a PR professional, "citable by an agent" is the autonomous-market equivalent of "quotable in a story." Just as a good spokesperson makes the reporter's job easier by providing clear, on-message quotes that can be used without paraphrase, a brand that is citable by an agent makes the agent's job easier by providing structured, verified, consistent information that can be retrieved and cited without ambiguity.

The properties that make a source citable by an agent overlap significantly with what makes good PR content:

Named, attributed claims (not anonymous assertions): Agents look for sourced information, the same thing a reporter wants.

Specific, verifiable statistics: A general claim is harder to cite than a specific figure with an identified source.

Consistent identity: A brand that uses its name, position, and core claims consistently across all its owned content is easier for an agent to resolve into a coherent identity, the same reason consistent PR messaging matters across media.

Fresh, frequently-updated content: Agents weight recency, the same reason PR teams work to keep a client's newsroom current.

The difference is in the delivery mechanism. Human reporters read and synthesize. Agents parse, retrieve, and structure. A press release optimized for human reading may be opaque to an agent. A knowledge base optimized for agent retrieval may be less engaging for a human reader. The best-positioned brands will develop both, and the PR professional who understands the agent-retrieval layer can help clients build it alongside traditional earned media programs.

## 3. Compounding Cohort Decay in the Autonomous Market

### How decay manifests here

In the autonomous market, cohort decay operates at machine speed. When an agent research query returns an answer, the brand that appears in that answer has earned a form of presence in that agent's working context. The brand that does not appear has been excluded from the consideration set before a human has seen anything.

The decay patterns from the aggregated market, Coverage Half-Life, Relationship Attrition, and Share-of-Voice Erosion, all appear in the autonomous market in accelerated form:

**Coverage Half-Life:** In autonomous markets, the half-life of content that an agent will cite shortens as competing, fresher content appears. An agent checking current sources for a brand recommendation will discount content that has not been updated recently, even if that content was once authoritative. The decay is faster than in human-mediated discovery because agents do not have the human tendency to remember a source they trusted last year.

**Relationship Attrition:** In autonomous markets, the "relationship" is between the brand's machine-readable presence and the agent's retrieval behavior. A brand whose API surface has not been updated, whose MCP server is inactive, or whose structured content has not been refreshed is in the equivalent position of a brand that has stopped returning reporters' calls. The agent will route around it.

**Share-of-Voice Erosion:** In the autonomous market, share of voice is measured in agent retrieval frequency. A brand that is cited in 30% of agent answers about its category today may find that percentage declining as competitors build more structured, more citable, more machine-readable presences. The erosion happens at algorithm speed, not at editorial cycle speed.

### The additional decay risk: trust-boundary exclusion

The autonomous market adds a decay pattern that does not exist in the aggregated or decentralized markets: trust-boundary exclusion. Agents are designed to treat external inputs with caution. A brand whose content contains elements that trigger an agent's safety filters, whether because of ambiguous claims, unverified statistics, or structural properties that look like potential injection vectors, may find itself excluded from agent citations entirely, not ranked lower but excluded. This is a binary failure mode that has no equivalent in traditional PR measurement.

## 4. Five PR/Comms Real-World Illustrations for the Autonomous Market

These examples use well-known events and dynamics that a communications professional covering technology, commerce, or consumer markets will recognize.

### Illustration 1: The ChatGPT Instant Checkout Launch

In September 2025, OpenAI and Stripe launched Instant Checkout inside ChatGPT, allowing users to purchase products from Etsy sellers and, in a rolling expansion, over a million Shopify merchants without leaving the chat interface. This was not a concept demonstration. It was a live commerce capability available to the more than 700 million weekly ChatGPT users.

For a PR professional, the ChatGPT Instant Checkout launch is the clearest signal that the "agent as buyer" scenario is no longer a future state. Merchants whose products were available through Shopify and compatible with the ACP standard had a form of discovery in the ChatGPT interface. Merchants that were not compatible did not. The distinction was not about brand equity or media presence; it was about whether the merchant's commerce interface was machine-readable and agent-callable.

The PR lesson: a brand's visibility in agent-mediated commerce is a function of its technical interface, not only its narrative. The PR professional who understands the interface layer can help clients recognize when their machine-readability gap is a competitive disadvantage that no amount of traditional media coverage will compensate for.

### Illustration 2: The Google Agent Payments Protocol Partner Announcement

On September 16, 2025, Google announced the Agent Payments Protocol with more than sixty payments and technology partners including Mastercard, American Express, PayPal, Coinbase, and Worldpay. The announcement was a signal from the incumbent financial infrastructure that agent-mediated payment was being treated as a real, near-term capability.

For a PR professional, the AP2 partner list is a case study in how open-protocol announcements work as PR events. The announcement's credibility came not from Google's statement but from the names on the partner list. When Mastercard and American Express co-sign a protocol announcement, the story is not a technology preview; it is an industry alignment signal. The journalists who covered AP2 were not covering a Google product; they were covering a shift in the payments industry's readiness for agent commerce.

The PR lesson: in autonomous markets, credibility travels through the partner ecosystem, not only through the brand's own communications. A brand that wants to establish credibility in agent-commerce positioning needs to be affiliated with, compliant with, or explicitly endorsed by the protocols and standards that the industry is rallying around.

### Illustration 3: Adobe's Machine-Readability Finding

Adobe's April 2026 analysis of more than a trillion visits to US retail sites found that "major portions of U.S. retail websites are not entirely readable by machines, which limits their visibility across AI search results." Adobe's own framing: "as the internet continues to be reintermediated by AI, there is increased urgency for retailers to revamp their digital properties."

For a PR professional, the Adobe finding is the most practically useful data point in the autonomous market research. It is not a forecast; it is a measurement. A large share of retail brand websites, brands with significant traditional media presence and strong earned coverage histories, are structurally invisible to the agents that are increasingly mediating consumer discovery and purchase.

The PR lesson: traditional media presence does not compensate for machine-readability gaps. A brand with a strong PR program and a non-machine-readable website is in the equivalent position of a brand with excellent print coverage that did not build a functional website when search became the primary discovery layer.

### Illustration 4: The Model Context Protocol Adoption Across Competing Platforms

When ChatGPT adopted Anthropic's Model Context Protocol in March 2025 ("OpenAI adopts rival Anthropic's standard for connecting AI models to data," per TechCrunch), it established MCP as the de facto open standard for agent-callable tools. By December 2025, MCP was in use across ChatGPT, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Cursor, and VS Code, with more than 97 million monthly SDK downloads and over 10,000 active public servers.

For a PR professional, the MCP adoption story is a case study in what happens when a technical standard crosses into mainstream infrastructure. The story is not that Anthropic built a good protocol. The story is that competing platforms chose to build on the same standard, which means any brand building an MCP-compatible interface is building on infrastructure that the industry has selected, not betting on a single vendor's future.

The PR lesson: the transition from a technical protocol to infrastructure is a communications event. The brands and agencies that understood the MCP adoption story when it happened, and began positioning agent-callable interfaces as a marketing capability, were ahead of the brands that waited for the trade press to normalize the concept. The same pattern will repeat for each successive agent-commerce standard.

### Illustration 5: The OWASP Prompt Injection Top 10 Publication

In December 2025, OWASP published a dedicated Top 10 for Agentic Applications, reviewed by NIST, the European Commission, and the Alan Turing Institute. OWASP ranks prompt injection as the number one risk to LLM applications, defining it as occurring when content retrieved by an agent carries hidden instructions that alter the agent's behavior.

For a PR professional, the OWASP publication is a crisis communications precursor. As agent-callable brand interfaces become more common, the possibility that a brand's public content contains elements that trigger agent safety filters, or worse, that a brand's interface is implicated in an indirect prompt injection incident, becomes a real reputational risk that requires pre-crisis planning.

The PR lesson: the trust-boundary discipline in agent-facing systems is not only a technical security matter. It is a reputational matter. A brand whose agent-callable interface is implicated in a security incident, even one where the brand was the victim rather than the cause, has a communications challenge that does not map cleanly onto traditional crisis PR playbooks. Pre-crisis planning for autonomous market reputation risks is a new service category that communications professionals should be developing.

## 5. MCP as the Distribution Layer PR Teams Will Need to Understand

### The wire service analogy

The research frames MCP as "the de facto open standard for exposing a system as an agent-callable tool." For a PR professional, the most useful analogy is a wire service.

A wire service (AP, Reuters, PR Newswire) is a distribution infrastructure that takes a brand's content and makes it accessible to a large number of news organizations simultaneously. The brand does not choose which outlets receive the wire content; the infrastructure distributes it to every subscriber. The brand's job is to make the content well-formatted and credible enough that outlets choose to use it.

MCP works similarly for agents. An MCP server makes a brand's knowledge, tools, and data accessible to every agent that supports the standard. The brand does not choose which agents query it; the infrastructure makes it available to all of them. The brand's job is to make the content well-formatted, structured, and trustworthy enough that agents choose to cite it.

For a PR professional, understanding MCP is not about understanding the technical protocol. It is about understanding that there is now a distribution infrastructure for brand information at the agent layer, analogous to the wire service infrastructure at the journalist layer. A brand that publishes an MCP-compatible interface has its knowledge in the agent distribution system. A brand that does not has not.

### The trust-boundary framework as PR ethics

The autonomous market's trust-boundary framework, treat every retrieved document as hostile input, sanitize everything that goes out, never let data become a command, is a security principle with a direct PR ethics translation.

In PR terms, the equivalent framework is: know the provenance of every claim your brand makes, ensure every output from your communications system is verifiably accurate, and never let a competitor's framing or a reporter's inaccurate paraphrase become the basis for a subsequent brand statement. These are principles that every good PR professional already holds. The autonomous market makes them technically enforceable at the system level rather than only at the human-review level.

## 6. Budget and Activation Framing for Comms Teams in Autonomous Markets

### The interface investment framing

For a PR team advising a client on autonomous-market readiness, the core investment framing is: the brand's machine-readable, agent-callable interface is the communications asset, and everything else, traditional media coverage, social presence, search ranking, is the validation layer that makes agents trust that interface.

**Labeled inputs:**

The Machine-Legibility Audit: The initial assessment of how much of the brand's current communications infrastructure, press room, newsroom, product documentation, policy pages, is structured for machine retrieval versus human reading. Adobe's research suggests this gap tends to be significant even for brands with strong traditional communications programs.

The Agent-Citability Investment: The ongoing cost of maintaining a structured, fresh, citable owned corpus that meets the structural criteria that make it retrievable by agents: named claims with sources, sequential headings, frequent updates, consistent brand voice and identity across all touchpoints.

The Trust-Boundary Preparation: The reputational risk management investment that addresses the new class of autonomous-market brand risks: agent safety filter exclusion, indirect prompt injection exposure, and the reputational consequences of a trust-boundary failure in an agent-callable system. This is not a technical security budget; it is a communications risk management budget that includes pre-crisis planning for scenarios that do not yet have established PR playbooks.

**What to stop renting:**

Campaign-burst content that is not structured for machine retrieval. In autonomous markets, a high-volume content campaign that generates impressive clip counts but produces no durable machine-readable assets is the communications equivalent of paying for impressions on a platform that the agent never visits. The output exists; the agent-citability does not.

**The narrative model for a CFO:**

The Discovery Half-Life in Autonomous Markets: An agent's retrieval behavior is sensitive to content freshness in ways that human discovery is not. A brand that publishes a major piece of research and then does not update its owned knowledge base for six months tends to find that the research's agent-citability decays as newer, fresher content from competitors appears. The cost of that decay is being removed from the agent's consideration set for an increasing volume of the market's discovery and purchase decisions.

The Compounding Return on Machine-Legibility Investment: A brand that builds a structured, agent-callable owned presence is building an asset that grows in agent-discoverability as the brand accumulates more external validation (mentions, citations, third-party references). Unlike traditional PR campaigns, which generate discrete bursts of coverage that decay, a well-maintained machine-legible presence tends to accumulate citability over time.

## 7. Cross-Reference: How Autonomous Market Patterns Connect to the Other Two Markets

**Autonomous to Aggregated:** The agent-citability requirement in autonomous markets is the accelerated, machine-speed version of the AI share of voice requirement in aggregated markets. A brand that has built a structured, citable owned corpus for aggregated market optimization has done the majority of the foundational work required for autonomous market machine-legibility.

**Autonomous to Decentralized:** In crypto-powered decentralized markets, agents transacting in stablecoins via x402 and AgentKit need to query a brand's onchain identity to verify its legitimacy before transacting. A brand with a verifiable onchain identity, as documented in the decentralized-market research, is in a stronger position for autonomous-market agent discovery than one that exists only in traditional web infrastructure.

**The shared infrastructure:** All three markets reward the same foundational investment: a persistent, structured, citable brand identity and owned corpus that machines can retrieve, verify, and trust. The aggregated market measures this as AI share of voice. The decentralized market measures this as onchain verifiability. The autonomous market measures this as agent-callability. The investment that serves all three is the same investment.
