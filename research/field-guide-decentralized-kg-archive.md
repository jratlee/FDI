## Executive Summary

A company's knowledge does not live in one place. It is scattered across documents, product data, support logs, contracts, and policies that do not talk to each other, and the cost of that fragmentation is now measurable rather than anecdotal. Coveo's 2025 EX Relevance Report, a survey of 4,000 workers at 5,000-plus-employee firms, found that 47% of employees name information stored across multiple applications as their single largest productivity hurdle, that the average worker loses roughly three hours a day to searching, that 42% of the information they sift through is irrelevant to their role, and that a typical search spans four separate data sources [1]. The oldest and most-quoted version of this problem, McKinsey Global Institute's finding that knowledge workers spend 1.8 hours a day (9.3 hours a week) searching and gathering information, is now more than a decade old but still directionally intact [9]. And the raw material keeps getting harder to reach: IDC's widely cited Data Age projection puts unstructured data at roughly 80% of all data, the fraction least amenable to a simple database query [8].

The thesis of the "Decentralized" Field Guide is that the winners will stitch those fragments into one owned, queryable knowledge graph, with the relationships between them preserved and citable. The evidence for the mechanism is strong and increasingly mainstream. Gartner named GraphRAG a top data-and-analytics trend for 2026 and predicts that 40% of enterprises will have leveraged GraphRAG techniques by 2029, arguing verbatim that "traditional retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) approaches cannot handle complex, context-rich queries" and that combining knowledge graphs with LLMs delivers "more accurate results" [10]. Peer-reviewed work backs this up: a hybrid vector-plus-graph pipeline achieved the highest and most stable factual correctness across easy-to-hard queries in a controlled benchmark [18], and a 12-author systematic evaluation found that graph methods win on multi-hop and global "sensemaking" questions while integration strategies that combine vector and graph retrieval yield consistent improvements [17]. A LinkedIn production system that grounded RAG in a knowledge graph cut median customer-service resolution time by 28.6% and improved retrieval quality (MRR) by 77.6% [11].

The second half of the thesis, "retrieval you own beats targeting you rent," is where the marketing argument lands. Grounding an answer in a structured, owned graph is also what makes the answer *checkable*: peer-reviewed research establishes traceability and verifiability as the explicit purpose of evidence-based generation [27], while Stanford found that even purpose-built legal RAG tools still hallucinate 17% to 34% of the time, proving that citations users can verify are not optional [29]. On the strategic side, S&P Global Ratings concluded in February 2026 that "established platform leaders with deep domain expertise and proprietary data are best positioned to navigate disruption from AI-native entrants" [32]. The nuance the writer must preserve is that the moat is not hoarded data but owned, continuously enriched, activated retrieval: several sources correctly note that raw data possession is a weak moat [37][38], which actually strengthens the "retrieval you own" framing over a naive "data you have" one. This is exactly what the Talk to NYC build demonstrates: it turns thousands of scattered legal XML files into one graph, answers with both vector search and citation-preserving graph traversal, keeps section numbers attached so the work can be checked, and exposes the whole thing as a tool other agents can call.

## Background

For most of the database era, connected knowledge was stored in tables and reassembled at query time through joins. That worked when the questions were simple and the relationships were shallow. It works poorly now, for two reasons that compound. First, the volume and heterogeneity of enterprise knowledge exploded: large organizations run hundreds of separate applications, and the majority of what they generate is unstructured text, images, logs, and documents rather than neatly typed rows [4][8]. Second, the interface changed. People and, increasingly, AI agents now ask questions in natural language and expect a synthesized, sourced answer, not a list of documents to read. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) emerged to bridge that gap by fetching relevant text and grounding a model's answer in it, but plain vector RAG retrieves isolated chunks by semantic similarity and has no representation of how one fact relates to another [17][18].

A knowledge graph stores those relationships as first-class, typed edges (`cites`, `caused_by`, `reports_to`, `contains`) rather than inferring them through joins. Its defining technical property, index-free adjacency, means local traversal performance depends only on the relationships at the nodes being visited, not on the total size of the graph, whereas n-way relational joins tend to degrade sharply as the number of joins grows [15][16]. GraphRAG and hybrid retrieval combine the two: vector search for meaning, graph traversal for the explicit connections between facts. The "Decentralized" thesis argues that this consolidated, owned graph, rather than the scattered fragments feeding rented ad and search platforms, is the durable asset. This report assembles the evidence for that argument across five focus areas: the cost of fragmentation, knowledge graphs as the owned structure, GraphRAG and hybrid retrieval accuracy, citation and provenance, and the owned-retrieval-layer moat. Sources span primary academic venues (KDD, SIGIR, EMNLP, arXiv), analyst firms (Gartner, Grand View Research, S&P Global), primary consumer/enterprise research (Coveo, McKinsey), and vendor benchmarks that are used only as clearly-attributed illustrations.

## Key Findings

### Finding 1: Fragmented, siloed knowledge is a large and measurable tax on the enterprise

The cost of scattered knowledge is documented from the demand side, by workers, in current, methodologically transparent research. Coveo's 2025 EX Relevance Report surveyed 4,000 workers at organizations of 5,000-plus employees and found that 47% cite information stored across multiple applications as their single biggest barrier to productivity, that the same share report that fragmented knowledge and disconnected tools cause burnout or frustration (up twelve points year over year), that employees lose about three hours a day to searching, that 42% of the information they wade through is irrelevant to their role, and that a typical search touches four different data sources [1][2]. Coveo also found that roughly half of workers had hit AI hallucinations at work, which links fragmentation directly to the "garbage in" problem for enterprise AI [1]. An independent intranet analysis reached the same three-hours-a-day figure by citing the Coveo report, indicating the number is being adopted as a reference point rather than an outlier [6].

Corroborating structure comes from older but reputable research. APQC's survey of 982 knowledge workers found that a typical worker spends only about 30 of 40 weekly hours on productive work, with 2.8 hours a week spent looking for or requesting needed information, 3.6 hours managing internal communication, and nearly 60% reporting lower job satisfaction as a result [3]. A Forrester study commissioned by Airtable found knowledge workers lose roughly 30% of their time looking for data and that large organizations run an average of 367 software applications, quantifying the sprawl that fragmentation lives in [4]. Both should be flagged as older than the 18-month freshness window (APQC is 2021; the Forrester/Airtable figure traces to roughly 2022), so they are best used as corroboration for the current Coveo numbers rather than as headline stats. The most famous figure of all, McKinsey Global Institute's estimate that employees spend 1.8 hours a day (9.3 hours a week) searching and gathering information, popularized as "you hire five workers but only four show up; the fifth is off looking for answers," comes from the 2012 report *The Social Economy* and is now well over a decade old [9]; it should be presented as a canonical, directional framing, not as fresh data.

Two things are worth stating honestly. First, the raw material is getting harder, not easier: IDC's Data Age projection that unstructured data will reach roughly 80% of all data is now essentially present-day and originates from older IDC forecasts, but it is consistent across sources and captures why simple queries fail on modern enterprise knowledge [8]. Second, several eye-catching dollar totals that circulate for this problem (a "$3.1 trillion a year" global cost, a "$7.8M per organization" figure, a "$450B a year" context-switching cost) could not be traced to a primary report and appear only in AI-generated search summaries; they should not be cited without locating the originals [see Limitations]. The defensible version of Finding 1 rests on the worker-reported time cost, which is well sourced and current.

### Finding 2: Knowledge graphs are the owned structure that makes scattered, connected knowledge queryable and citable

If fragmentation is the disease, a consolidated knowledge graph is the structural cure, and the shift from niche to mainstream is now on the record from the most influential analyst in the category. Gartner named GraphRAG a top data-and-analytics trend for 2026 and predicts that 40% of enterprises will have leveraged GraphRAG techniques by 2029 to improve the factual accuracy and reasoning of LLMs, stating verbatim that "traditional retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) approaches cannot handle complex, context-rich queries" and that "GraphRAG combines knowledge graphs with LLMs to improve how AI systems retrieve and connect information, apply contextual meaning and deliver more accurate results" [10]. Gartner separately predicts that more than one in ten enterprises will be "AI-first" by 2030, with semantics and knowledge structures as a driving force [10]. The market forecasts, while widely dispersed, all point up and to the right: Grand View Research sizes the enterprise knowledge graph market at about $2.9B in 2025 growing to $13.4B by 2033 (21.3% CAGR), while Research & Markets projects roughly $1.5B in 2025 to $8.9B by 2032 (about 29% CAGR) [12][13]. The roughly ten-fold spread between firms is itself a finding: it reflects inconsistent definitions of "knowledge graph," so any single market number should be cited as a range with attribution, never as a settled figure.

The reason a graph beats a flat or relational store for connected knowledge is technical and non-controversial across even competing vendors. Index-free adjacency means each node directly references its neighbors, so traversing local relationships costs the same regardless of how large the overall graph grows; relational databases, by contrast, store relationships as foreign keys resolved through joins at query time, and multi-way joins tend to degrade, often exponentially, as their number increases [15][16]. Because relationships are stored as explicit, typed edges, they are preserved and inspectable, which is precisely the "relationships preserved and citable" property the Decentralized thesis calls for [15].

The grounding benefit is quantified, though the strongest case studies carry age flags. LinkedIn's production RAG-plus-knowledge-graph system for customer service, published at SIGIR 2024, cut median issue-resolution time by 28.6% and improved retrieval MRR by 77.6% in a system deployed for about six months [11] (note: April 2024, older than 18 months, but peer-reviewed and directly on point). A data.world benchmark across 43 business questions on a real insurance SQL schema found that adding a knowledge graph roughly tripled LLM answer accuracy, with the LLM-alone baseline averaging 16.7% and scoring 0% on schema-intensive KPI, metrics, and strategic-planning questions [14] (a vendor benchmark from November 2023, useful as an illustration, not neutral proof). The mechanism behind these gains is consistent: a graph grounds answers in structured facts and supplies provenance, so if an edge does not exist the retriever cannot supply it, which both cuts fabrication and lets a human audit each assertion against its source [10][14].

### Finding 3: GraphRAG and hybrid vector-plus-graph retrieval measurably beat vector-only RAG on the questions that matter

The most rigorous, on-thesis evidence comes from peer-reviewed and arXiv research comparing retrieval architectures, and its verdict is nuanced in a way that helps rather than hurts the argument. Microsoft Research introduced GraphRAG in the paper "From Local to Global," which showed that plain RAG fails on global questions directed at an entire corpus ("what are the main themes?") because that is a summarization task, not a retrieval task; GraphRAG builds an entity knowledge graph, detects communities, and pre-generates summaries so it can answer both local and global questions at scale [21]. The most directly analogous study to the Talk to NYC build is a July 2025 benchmark on structured, cross-referencing technical specifications, in which hybrid GraphRAG (running vector similarity and graph traversal together and fusing the results) achieved the highest factual correctness (0.58) across all difficulty levels, versus roughly 0.50 for both graph-only and vector-only RAG; crucially, vector RAG did best on *easy* questions but degraded on medium and hard ones, while the hybrid stayed stable by compensating for each method's weaknesses [18]. This is the cleanest support for a hybrid legal or specification system that must traverse how one rule cites another.

A 12-author systematic evaluation (Michigan State, Meta, IBM) reinforced the "it depends, so combine them" conclusion: plain RAG wins on single-hop, detail-level questions; GraphRAG wins on multi-hop reasoning and global sensemaking; and integration strategies that combine both consistently improve results [17]. Multi-hop question answering, the category that most resembles following a chain of legal citations, is where graph reasoning shines: StepChain GraphRAG reached state of the art on HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and MuSiQue, with the largest gain on HotpotQA (+4.70% exact match, +3.44% F1) [19]. LightRAG, accepted to EMNLP 2025, showed a dual-level graph retriever winning decisively on answer diversity (59.2% to 77.2% win rates against baselines) while indexing far more cheaply than Microsoft's original GraphRAG [20], and Microsoft's own LazyGraphRAG reframed the cost problem by deferring LLM use to query time, achieving graph-quality answers at indexing cost close to vanilla vector RAG [23].

Vendor benchmarks report far more dramatic numbers and should be used only as clearly-attributed illustrations, never as neutral fact. A Diffbot benchmark (re-cited by FalkorDB) reported LLM accuracy rising from 16.7% without knowledge-graph grounding to 56.2% with it, roughly 3.4x, and claimed vector RAG collapsed to 0% on certain schema-bound query categories while a graph sustained performance [24]. A production case study from Cognilium on a four-million-document corpus reported citation accuracy improving from 87% to 96% and multi-hop correctness from 41% to 78%, at roughly 1.6x cost and a modest latency increase [25], and an AIMultiple study on Amazon-review data found graph RAG answered aggregation queries correctly 73.5% of the time versus 18.5% for vector RAG, though vector RAG retrieved specific documents better [26]. The honest, defensible framing for the Field Guide is not a single magic percentage, because gains are strongly query-type-dependent (large for multi-hop, aggregation, and global questions; neutral or even negative for simple single-hop lookups). The Tier-1 claim to lead with is that hybrid retrieval delivers the most *stable* factual correctness across the full range of question difficulty, which is exactly the profile of an owned graph you have to trust across every kind of question a customer or agent might ask [18][17].

### Finding 4: Traceable, citable answers beat black-box answers, and grounding alone is not enough

The Talk to NYC mechanic of keeping section numbers attached to every answer is not a nicety; it addresses a documented reliability failure and an emerging compliance requirement. The core finding from the attribution research is that grounding reduces hallucination but does not eliminate it. Stanford's RegLab and HAI benchmarked purpose-built, RAG-powered legal research tools and found that LexisNexis and Thomson Reuters products still produced incorrect information between 17% and 34% of the time, far better than general-purpose chatbots (which hallucinated on 58% to 82% of legal queries in an earlier Stanford study) but nowhere near reliable enough to trust without checking [29] (note: the underlying study is May 2024, older than 18 months, and would benefit from a refreshed benchmark). The unavoidable conclusion is that if the model can still be wrong up to a third of the time even when grounded, the answer must carry a citation the reader can verify, which is precisely the design of a citation-preserving graph.

The academic field around this is real, active, and directly supportive of the thesis. An August 2025 survey systematically analyzed 134 papers and 300 evaluation metrics and defined the explicit purpose of evidence-based generation as linking model outputs to supporting evidence "to ensure traceability and verifiability," while noting the field remains fragmented for lack of unified benchmarks [27]. A peer-reviewed 2025 paper (ACM ICTIR) sharpened the standard further by distinguishing citation *correctness* from citation *faithfulness*, the question of whether a model genuinely relied on the cited source rather than retrofitting a citation to knowledge it already had, and argued that measuring correctness alone is insufficient [28]. Complementary work such as VeriCite proposes rigorous claim-level verification of citations before they are shown [30]. Together these establish that "cite your source" is becoming an engineerable, testable property, not a cosmetic footnote.

Provenance is also hardening into a regulatory obligation, which turns a design choice into a business requirement. Data-lineage tooling is being marketed explicitly around "immutable, regulator-ready audit trails" and "proof of lawful AI use," recording exactly which datasets a system retrieved for a given prompt, conceptually identical to attaching section numbers to an answer [31]. Regulatory drivers cited across the research include the EU AI Act's documented-provenance requirements for high-risk systems (with enforcement phasing in through August 2026) and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework's treatment of provenance and lineage as safety-critical artifacts [31]. Several striking business-cost figures for un-citable AI (a Deloitte claim that 47% of enterprise AI users made a major decision on hallucinated content, an EY figure of $4.4M average AI-related losses, and specific 2026 court-sanction totals) surfaced only in search-answer synthesis and are flagged for primary verification before publication [see Limitations]. The verified core is enough to carry the argument: traceable answers are demonstrably more trustworthy, the standards for measuring citation quality are maturing, and provenance is moving from best practice to legal expectation.

### Finding 5: An owned retrieval layer is a durable competitive asset, while rented targeting is structurally unstable

The strategic half of the thesis, "retrieval you own beats targeting you rent," is supported by an unusually authoritative source. S&P Global Ratings, an independent credit-ratings authority, concluded in February 2026 that "established platform leaders with deep domain expertise and proprietary data are best positioned to navigate disruption from AI-native entrants," while undifferentiated point solutions face margin pressure and displacement, and that traditional software moats are being challenged as AI capability commoditizes [32]. A Forbes analysis of venture investors reframed the same idea usefully: AI compresses the time it takes to *build* things but not the time for things to *happen*, so defensibility now comes from assets that required elapsed, real-world time to accumulate, with "living, compounding proprietary data" generated through operations as the first surviving moat [33]. Trade analysis makes the mechanism concrete: once LLM APIs flatten model access, "the model is no longer the differentiator; it's the infrastructure wrapped around it," and durable advantage shifts to proprietary, high-quality data and the systems that keep learning from it [35].

The instability of rented targeting is best illustrated by the third-party cookie saga. After years of promising to deprecate third-party cookies in Chrome (which holds roughly two-thirds of the global browser market), Google reversed course, announcing in July 2024 that it would not phase them out as planned and confirming in April 2025 that they would stay [36]. That whipsaw is the point: strategies built on infrastructure a platform controls can be rewritten by that platform overnight, and the regulatory ground keeps shifting, with 19 US states now enforcing comprehensive privacy laws; treating the cookie reversal "as permission to delay" first-party data investment is described as a costly mistake [34]. The prudent response, consistent across sources, is to move value onto assets you own.

The most important nuance, and the one that keeps the argument credible, is that raw data possession is a weak moat. Skeptical sources argue persuasively that many claimed data moats are "porous or illusory" because organizations "confuse data possession with data leverage," and that piggybacking on ever-stronger LLMs erodes the value of merely having a dataset [37][38]. Far from undermining the thesis, this sharpens it: the defensible asset is not a data lake you hoard but an owned, continuously enriched, *activated* retrieval layer, a structured graph that a model (yours or a customer's agent) can query, traverse, and cite. That is the difference between "data you have" and "retrieval you own." One area the open web covered thinly is the emerging discipline of getting your owned corpus cited by AI assistants, variously called Generative Engine Optimization or Answer Engine Optimization, which is now a recognized 2026 practice for making brand content citable inside model answers [39][40]; it is a natural bridge between this guide and the Aggregated guide, and reinforces that an owned, machine-retrievable corpus is the substrate both markets reward.

## Analysis

Read together, the five findings describe one arc: knowledge is scattered and that scattering is expensive (Finding 1); a consolidated, owned knowledge graph is the structure that makes scattered, connected knowledge queryable and citable (Finding 2); hybrid vector-plus-graph retrieval over that graph measurably outperforms vector-only RAG on exactly the multi-hop, cross-referencing questions enterprises actually ask (Finding 3); grounding answers in that graph is also what makes them traceable, and traceability is both a proven reliability lever and a hardening compliance requirement (Finding 4); and owning that retrieval layer is a durable competitive asset in a way that renting targeting from platforms is not (Finding 5). The Talk to NYC build sits at the center of all five: it aggregates thousands of scattered legal XML files into one graph (answering Finding 1's fragmentation), preserves the citation relationships as traversable edges (Finding 2), answers with both vector search and graph traversal (Finding 3), keeps section numbers attached so every answer can be checked (Finding 4), and exposes the graph through an MCP server as a tool other agents can call (which extends the owned-asset logic of Finding 5 into the Autonomous market).

The cross-cutting insight is that the two properties an owned graph provides, *connection* and *citation*, are the two that both the accuracy research and the trust research independently demand. The accuracy literature says the wins come on multi-hop and global questions, which are precisely the questions that require traversing relationships a vector index throws away [17][18][19]. The trust literature says answers must be verifiable, which requires that the relationships and their sources be preserved and inspectable [27][28][29]. A knowledge graph is the one structure that satisfies both at once, and it happens to be an asset a company can own rather than rent. This is why the Decentralized thesis is not merely a technical preference; it is the same "own the durable substrate" logic that ran through the first Field Guide's "identity as the ultimate infrastructure," applied to knowledge.

The counter-evidence disciplines rather than defeats the argument, and the guide is stronger for engaging it. GraphRAG is not universally better: it can underperform vector RAG on simple single-hop lookups and it adds indexing cost, storage, and latency [17][23][25]. Market-size estimates disagree by an order of magnitude [12][13]. The strongest quantified case studies (LinkedIn 2024, data.world 2023, McKinsey 2012, Stanford 2024) are older than the freshness window and should be paired with 2025-2026 corroboration [9][11][14][29]. And the moat is not raw data but activated retrieval [37][38]. None of these dents the core claim; each simply narrows it to its defensible form: *hybrid retrieval over an owned, citable graph wins on the connected, high-stakes questions that matter, and that owned graph is a durable asset in a way rented targeting is not.* The volatility of the rented layer, a cookie policy reversed twice in a year [36], a platform that can rewrite the rules of reach at will, is itself the argument for investing in the owned substrate instead.

## Limitations

Several caveats bound these findings. First, the most dramatic dollar figures for both the cost of fragmentation ($3.1T, $7.8M per organization, $450B context-switching) and the cost of un-citable AI (Deloitte 47%, EY $4.4M, specific 2026 court sanctions) surfaced only through AI-generated search summaries and could not be traced to primary reports; they are excluded from the findings and must be primary-sourced before any publication uses them. Second, key quantified case studies are older than the 18-month freshness window: McKinsey's search-time stat is 2012 [9], the LinkedIn KG result is April 2024 [11], the data.world benchmark is November 2023 [14], and Stanford's legal-hallucination study is May 2024 [29]; all remain directionally valid but should be paired with current corroboration. Third, the most striking accuracy multipliers (Diffbot 3.4x, FalkorDB 90%+, Cognilium 87%→96%) are vendor benchmarks testing their own products and are used only as attributed illustrations, not neutral proof [24][25]. Fourth, enterprise knowledge-graph market sizes vary roughly ten-fold across analyst firms due to differing definitions [12][13]. Fifth, no published GraphRAG accuracy benchmark exists specifically for the legal-citation domain of Talk to NYC; the structured-specification (ORAN) hybrid benchmark is the closest transferable proxy [18]. Finally, several secondary Gartner figures relayed by vendors could not be verified on gartner.com; only the "40% by 2029" GraphRAG prediction was confirmed against the primary release [10].

## Recommendations

For the writer of the "Decentralized" Field Guide, the research supports a confident, defensible argument built on the following moves.

**Open with the worker-reported cost of fragmentation, not a shaky trillion-dollar total.** Lead with Coveo's current, methodologically sound numbers, three hours a day lost to searching, 47% blocked by information scattered across apps, four data sources per search [1], and use McKinsey's "the fifth employee is off looking for answers" as the memorable framing while noting its age [9]. This makes the problem concrete without resting on unverifiable aggregate figures.

**Name the structure and cite the mainstream turn.** State plainly that the fix is one owned, queryable knowledge graph, and anchor its legitimacy in Gartner's 2026 prediction that 40% of enterprises will use GraphRAG by 2029 and its verbatim admission that plain RAG "cannot handle complex, context-rich queries" [10]. Explain index-free adjacency versus join degradation as the reason graphs preserve relationships that flat retrieval throws away [15][16].

**Prove the accuracy claim with the honest, stable-correctness framing.** Do not promise a single magic percentage. Lead with the Tier-1 result that hybrid vector-plus-graph retrieval delivers the most *stable* factual correctness across easy-to-hard queries [18] and wins on multi-hop and global questions [17][19], and present dramatic vendor numbers only as clearly-labeled illustrations [24]. This will read as credible to sophisticated readers who know GraphRAG is not universally better.

**Make traceability the trust argument, and Talk to NYC the proof.** Use Stanford's finding that even purpose-built legal RAG still hallucinates 17-34% [29] to show why answers must be checkable, then present Talk to NYC's section-number citations and citation-preserving graph traversal as the working answer, reinforced by the peer-reviewed traceability/faithfulness literature [27][28] and the compliance pressure from the EU AI Act and NIST [31].

**Land the strategic thesis and tie it to the series.** Close on "retrieval you own beats targeting you rent," backed by S&P Global's proprietary-data-as-defensibility conclusion [32] and the twice-reversed cookie saga as proof that rented infrastructure is unstable [36]. Preserve the crucial nuance that the moat is activated retrieval, not hoarded data [37][38], and hand off to the Autonomous guide via Talk to NYC's MCP server, the point where an owned, citable graph becomes something another agent can safely call.
