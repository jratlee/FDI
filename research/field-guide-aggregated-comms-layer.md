## 1. Why This Appendix Exists

The aggregated-market research documents a structural shift in how discovery works: AI answer engines now sit between brands and audiences, absorbing the click, and the metrics PR teams have relied on for decades, clip counts, average ad value equivalency, share of voice in print and broadcast, are being measured against a landscape that is quietly reorganizing underneath them.

This appendix does not replace the research. It adds a translation layer: what do the aggregated-market findings mean for someone who earns their living building and protecting earned media presence, managing journalist relationships, and measuring a brand's coverage footprint? It identifies the specific PR/comms KPIs where the decay is already visible, names five well-known real-world illustrations that a non-technical PR professional will recognize immediately, and lays out the budget and activation framing that the field guide outline will need.

## 2. The PR/Comms Consequence of AI-Mediated Discovery

### What the research says in plain language

Pew Research observed that when an AI-generated summary appears in a Google search, only about 1% of users click any cited source. Bain found that roughly 60% of searches now end without a click to any website. Global publisher traffic from Google fell an estimated third in the year to November 2025.

For a PR team, the translation is direct: the coverage you earned, the article that ran in a major outlet, the blog post that quoted your CEO, may still exist and still rank. But if it is summarized inside an AI answer, the reader may never arrive at the page. The clip exists. The visit does not. The measurement your program has reported on for years, coverage that drives traffic, referrals, and lead attribution, has a new leak in it that most measurement dashboards do not yet show.

This is not a reason to panic, and it is not a reason to abandon earned media. It is a reason to understand what is changing and to start measuring the new signal alongside the old one.

### The new KPI landscape

**Traditional PR KPIs and where they are being affected:**

Coverage volume (clip count): Still measurable and still meaningful as a proxy for narrative presence. The risk is that clip count increasingly measures content creation rather than audience delivery. A story can be placed, indexed, and summarized by an AI without generating a single human visit to the source.

Average ad value equivalency (AVE): Already a contested metric in the profession. AI-mediated discovery accelerates its obsolescence because AVE assumes the coverage was seen by a human who could have otherwise seen a paid ad. If the AI answer absorbs the impression, the advertising-value comparison has no referent.

Share of voice in traditional coverage: Remains a valid measure of narrative presence in the journalist and editor ecosystem. Requires pairing with AI share of voice measurement to give a complete picture.

**Emerging KPIs the aggregated market is creating:**

AI share of voice: How often a brand appears in AI-generated answers to relevant queries. Tooling exists (HubSpot's AEO Grader among others). Brands that treat this as a budgeted KPI are early movers.

Citation persistence: Whether a brand's owned or earned content stays cited across multiple AI queries over time. AirOps research suggests only 30% of brands stay visible from one answer to the next, and just 20% remain across five consecutive runs. Citation persistence is the AI equivalent of durable search ranking.

Earned coverage citability score: Whether the coverage a PR team places meets the structural criteria that make it citable by AI: quotes and statistics, original research, sequential headings, schema markup, fresh publication or update dates. A clip that earns a citation in an AI answer has a longer shelf life than one that does not.

## 3. Compounding Cohort Decay Mapped to PR/Comms KPIs

### The concept in PR/comms terms

Compounding cohort decay is the pattern where the value generated by any given marketing effort, a campaign, a media push, a coverage wave, decays over time unless it is actively reinforced by owned investment. In traditional cohort economics, a company acquires customers and each cohort gradually churns unless retention is funded. In PR terms, the cohort is the audience attention a coverage wave generates: the readers who learned about a brand during a major media moment, the journalists who wrote the brand's number into their mental model of the space, the algorithm signals that elevated the brand's search presence for a period.

All three of those decay. How fast depends on whether the brand has built owned infrastructure to slow the decay, or whether it is renting all of its presence from the media and the platforms.

### Coverage Half-Life

**What it is:** Every piece of earned coverage has a period during which it actively drives discovery, citation, and referral. Before AI-mediated discovery, the half-life of good coverage was limited mostly by how quickly a story moved off the front page and fell in search rankings. Now a second force accelerates decay: AI answers can summarize coverage without returning traffic to it, which means the original article gradually stops being a traffic-generating asset even if it stays indexed and cited.

**How it compounds:** A PR team that runs a major campaign, earns 200 clips, and then shifts focus to the next push does not just lose the direct traffic benefit over time. It loses the citation benefit as AI answers update their training and begin to treat the coverage as dated. The brand's AI share of voice, built during the campaign period, tends to erode unless the underlying evidence base is kept fresh.

**Real-world illustration:** The pattern of a technology company announcing a major product, earning extensive coverage during launch week, and then finding that its share of AI-generated answers about the category gradually drifts toward competitors who have published more recent, structured, frequently-updated content. The launch coverage exists. The ongoing citability belongs to whoever kept publishing.

### Relationship Attrition

**What it is:** The decay of journalist and editor relationships over time when they are not maintained through genuine interaction, substantive briefings, and relevant story placement. In a PR context, relationship attrition is the slow erosion of a reporter's mental model of a brand as a reliable, relevant source.

**How it compounds:** A brand that invests heavily in a media push, builds strong journalist relationships during a news moment, and then goes quiet for two quarters tends to find that its media relationships have softened. Reporters move beats, shift focus, or simply form stronger relationships with brands that stayed in consistent contact. When the brand needs to re-enter coverage, it is often starting a relationship over rather than resuming one.

**The AI-era wrinkle:** AI-generated briefings and pitches are becoming common, and reporters tend to notice the volume shift. The brands that differentiate through genuine, personalized contact and substantive human relationship-building may find that relationship quality concentrates at the top, where human access is the differentiator. Relationship attrition tends to accelerate when contact is low-quality and high-volume rather than infrequent and substantive.

**Real-world illustration:** Any technology brand that built strong media relationships during a period of rapid growth, went through a quiet operational period, and then found that re-earning coverage required significantly more effort than the first wave. The relationship was not broken; it had simply decayed for lack of maintenance.

### Share-of-Voice Erosion

**What it is:** The gradual decline of a brand's presence in the ongoing media conversation about its category, relative to competitors who are actively investing in coverage, commentary, and owned content.

**How it compounds in the aggregated market:** In traditional media measurement, share of voice is measured in publication mentions relative to a defined competitor set. In the AI-mediated discovery environment, share of voice has a second dimension: how often the brand appears in AI-generated answers about the category relative to competitors. Brands with higher AI share of voice tend to be those with more structured, more frequently-updated, and more widely-cited owned content, not necessarily those that earned the most traditional media coverage.

The compound effect: a brand that focuses budget on earned media campaigns without investing in the owned content infrastructure that makes that coverage persistently citable may find its AI share of voice eroding even as its traditional clip count holds. The clips exist in the media record. The AI answers increasingly summarize competitors whose owned corpus is more machine-readable.

**Real-world illustration:** Any established PR consultancy's client in a technology or health category where newer, digitally native competitors have invested in structured content, FAQ pages, original research, and machine-readable owned assets finds that AI search results frequently surface the newcomers despite the incumbent's significantly larger traditional media footprint.

## 4. Five PR/Comms Real-World Illustrations for the Aggregated Market

These examples use well-known events and dynamics that a senior PR professional will recognize immediately. No former-client names are used.

### Illustration 1: The Wikipedia Citation Asymmetry

Semrush's analysis of AI citation patterns found that Wikipedia is the second-most-cited domain in AI answers, capturing 26.3% of citations across a 150,000-citation sample. Reddit ranked first at 40.1%. Most brands' owned websites do not appear in the top citation sources.

For a PR team, the implication is concrete: a brand's Wikipedia page is frequently more influential in AI-generated brand answers than the brand's own press room, newsroom, or owned blog. This is something PR professionals already intuitively understand about reputation management. The aggregated market makes it more urgent: keeping Wikipedia entries accurate, substantive, and well-sourced is no longer just a reputation-hygiene task. It is an AI-citation task.

### Illustration 2: The Chegg Traffic Collapse

Chegg, an education technology company, sued Google in February 2025, stating directly that it "depends on referrals from Google's monopoly search engine for a large portion of the revenue." Chegg's argument was that AI Overviews were absorbing the queries that had previously driven users to Chegg's pages. Chegg's stock fell to approximately $1 per share with a market capitalization below $200 million.

A PR team pitching a story about education, student tools, or edtech category dynamics to a business reporter needs to understand this case. It is one of the clearest documented examples of AI-mediated discovery directly affecting an organization's financial health. The Chegg case is not an abstraction: it is a publicly documented, financially measured instance of what happens when a brand's discovery model depends entirely on a platform it does not control.

### Illustration 3: The Penske Media "Coercive Bargain"

In September 2025, Penske Media (Rolling Stone, Variety, Billboard) became the first major U.S. news publisher to sue Google over AI Overviews. Penske alleged a "coercive bargain": allow your content into AI summaries or accept worse search rankings. Their argument was that they had no genuine choice in whether their journalism appeared in AI answers, because opting out would damage their organic search visibility.

For a PR professional whose clients rely on placements in Penske Media publications, this case illustrates that the publications themselves are navigating the same discovery-erosion pressure. A story placed in Rolling Stone or Variety now exists at two levels: the traditional coverage (which is still valuable for brand narrative and journalist relationships) and the AI-citation question (whether that story will be surfaced in AI answers about the brand's category).

### Illustration 4: The TikTok Following Feed Reality

Analysis of 31,059 TikTok posts with traffic-source data found that 85.1% of views arrive via the algorithmic For You page, while the Following feed, the feed of accounts a user deliberately chose to follow, delivers roughly 0.3% of views. A brand's TikTok followers are not a reach asset in any meaningful sense. They are an algorithmic signal.

For a PR professional advising a brand on social media strategy, this finding cuts to the core of a conversation many teams are having: the difference between building an audience and renting one. 300,000 TikTok followers does not mean 300,000 people will see the next post. It means 300,000 people told the algorithm they were once interested. The algorithm decides what happens next.

### Illustration 5: The GEO Benchmark Result

Researchers from Princeton, Georgia Tech, the Allen Institute for AI, and IIT Delhi published the foundational GEO paper at KDD 2024. Their controlled experiment found that content optimized for generative engine retrieval, through techniques including citing sources, adding statistics, and including quotations, improved visibility in AI-generated responses by up to 40% in the benchmark environment. The researchers introduced the term "Generative Engine Optimization" as the discipline for making content citable by AI.

For a PR professional, the GEO finding reframes a familiar practice: getting a client quoted in media coverage with specific data points, named claims, and attributed statistics improves the coverage's value not just for traditional audience impact but for AI citability. A CEO quote that includes a specific figure, a product claim that cites a study, a press release that attributes its central claim to original research, these are all GEO-optimized by design. The discipline builds on what good PR already does. The aggregated market makes it more consequential.

## 5. The "New Media Relations" Framing: GEO as a PR Discipline

One of the most important reframes for a PR audience is positioning GEO not as a technical SEO task but as the natural extension of media relations into the AI-mediated discovery layer.

Traditional media relations is the practice of making a brand's story findable, citable, and attractive to human journalists and editors. The infrastructure of traditional media relations includes: building a press room with structured, accurate information; maintaining a consistent record of attributed quotes and sourced claims; developing relationships with the journalists who cover the brand's category; and ensuring that when a reporter looks for a comment, a source, or a statistic, the brand's press room or spokesperson is the most reliable option they find.

GEO does the same work for AI answer engines. An AI answer engine looks for: structured content with clear headings, cited statistics and attributed claims, content that is fresh and frequently updated, and source authority that is corroborated by mentions and citations elsewhere on the web. These are precisely the properties a well-maintained PR program already builds into its owned content.

The practical implication: a PR team that already manages a brand newsroom, coordinates CEO commentary, and maintains a consistent record of attributed research is already doing proto-GEO. The aggregated market asks them to structure and maintain that owned corpus with the same discipline they bring to traditional media relations, and to measure its AI citability alongside its traditional coverage metrics.

## 6. AI Share of Voice as a PR KPI

AI share of voice is defined as how often a brand appears in AI-generated answers to relevant queries relative to its competitors. This is a new KPI that the aggregated market is creating, and it belongs in a PR program's measurement dashboard alongside traditional coverage metrics.

**How to explain AI share of voice to a client:** When a potential customer, a reporter researching a story, or an investor doing category diligence asks an AI assistant about the brand's space, how often does the brand appear in the answer? And how often does it appear relative to competitors? That is AI share of voice, and it tends to be correlated with the quality, freshness, and machine-readability of owned content more than with traditional media clip volume.

**How to explain it to a CFO:** AI share of voice is to AI-mediated discovery what organic search ranking was to search-engine-mediated discovery. A brand with a strong organic search presence earned that position through SEO investment over time. A brand with a strong AI share of voice earns that position through GEO investment over time: structured, fresh, well-cited owned content. The difference is that AI share of voice is still early enough that brands can build a durable position before the space is as competitive as organic search rankings.

**The incumbency caution:** Research from a June 2026 arXiv study found that when competing products have identical specifications, well-known incumbent brands tend to be recommended significantly more often by AI models. This suggests that AI share of voice, like traditional share of voice, tends to favor established brands in the early period. However, the same research found this incumbency advantage disappears once a competitor holds even a small rating advantage. The window to build AI share of voice before it becomes a fully competitive space may be relatively short.

## 7. Budget and Activation Framing for Comms Teams

### Approach A: Aligning comms investment with evolving technology and marketplace dynamics

This framing positions the owned-system investment as a natural evolution of what comms teams already do, rather than a wholesale new capability.

**What to build now:** A structured brand newsroom with machine-readable owned content (FAQ pages, executive commentary, original research with attributed statistics, press releases with schema markup). A GEO audit of the brand's current owned content to identify which pages are citable and which are not. A measurement baseline for AI share of voice, so that future investment can be tracked against a starting point.

**What to stop renting at the margins:** Clip-count-only PR measurement that does not distinguish between coverage that generates visits and coverage that exists but is summarized away by AI. Social media reach that is built on follower counts that the algorithm does not honor. Campaign-burst coverage that creates a temporary spike but does not build a persistent owned corpus.

**The labeled inputs for a CFO narrative:**

The Attrition Tax: The budget required to recreate media momentum from scratch when each campaign's coverage decays without an owned corpus to anchor it. This is the ongoing cost of the rented-reach model, paid out as campaign-to-campaign reinvestment.

The Compounding Return on Owned Corpus Investment: The growing value of structured, fresh, cited owned content that AI systems can retrieve and surface. Unlike a campaign's coverage, which decays, a well-maintained owned corpus tends to compound in citability over time as it accumulates more external mentions and AI training references.

The Citation Surface: The total owned and earned content that is currently machine-readable and AI-citable. This is the measurable asset the comms team is building. It grows as the team publishes structured content and earns citations, and it decays when content ages without updates.

### Approach B: Methodically building long-lasting brand success

This framing is appropriate for clients with longer planning horizons or who are skeptical of short-term AI trends.

The core argument is that the aggregated market is the amplified version of a shift that has been underway for over a decade: the erosion of organic reach as a reliable distribution channel. Any PR program that relies primarily on earned media to drive discovery is exposed to the same structural pressure, whether the intermediary is a social algorithm, a search engine, or an AI answer engine.

The owned-system investment is not a bet on AI specifically. It is a bet on the durable value of a brand asset that the intermediary cannot substitute away: a persistent identity, a verified corpus of trustworthy information, and the structural properties (freshness, citation, machine-readability) that make that corpus citable by whatever discovery layer exists in three years.

**Narrative model labeled inputs:**

The Owned Identity Substrate: The brand's persistent, structured, citable presence that exists independent of any platform. Analogous to the equity value of a strong media brand versus a social media account with the same reach.

The Earned Validation Layer: The off-site mentions, journalist relationships, and third-party citations that corroborate the owned corpus and help overcome AI incumbency bias. This is the PR team's traditional work, repositioned as the validation layer that makes the owned corpus trustworthy.

The Discovery Half-Life: The measurable period during which a coverage burst or campaign generates AI citations. Shortening the discovery half-life is the risk of rented reach; lengthening it through owned infrastructure investment is the return model.

## 8. Cross-Reference: How Aggregated Market Patterns Connect to the Other Two Markets

**Aggregated to Decentralized:** In decentralized markets, the knowledge fragmentation problem maps directly to the owned corpus problem in aggregated markets. A brand whose knowledge is scattered across documents, support logs, and policy pages that do not talk to each other is structurally invisible to both AI answer engines and decentralized protocol discovery. The Pile and knowledge-graph investments are the same answer to two versions of the same question.

**Aggregated to Autonomous:** When agents transact on behalf of users, they are using AI-mediated discovery at machine speed and machine scale. A brand with a strong AI share of voice in the aggregated market has already done much of the work required to be discoverable and citable to agents in the autonomous market. The owned corpus is the shared infrastructure.
