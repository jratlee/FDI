# Blueprint 04: The Audit to Kill Checklist
### Defragmenting QBRs, status meetings, and the review layers that eat your margin

**FDI MarCom OS, Foundation Playbook V1.0. As of 2026.**

---

## 1. The method

Defined once: Audit to Kill is a structured pass over every recurring process in an account or department that ends in a named list of processes that die, each replaced by a shifted-left check inside the Riverbank (Blueprint 03) or by nothing at all. It is not an efficiency review. Efficiency reviews produce recommendations; this produces a kill list with names and dates.

The single diagnostic question, applied to every step: **does this step exist to improve the work, or to reassure someone that work happened?** Improvement steps stay (and usually move earlier). Reassurance steps die, and the reassurance need gets met by transparency instead: visible gates, visible logs, visible rules.

The economics: in an AI-throughput shop, production time is no longer where the hours are. The hours are in the waiting: assets queuing for tiered reviews, teams assembling status decks about the queue, meetings scheduled to discuss the deck. Killing the wrapper is the fastest margin action available to an agency this year, and it requires the Riverbank to be in place first. Kill the meetings before the gates exist and you have traded latency for exposure.

## 2. The inventory (60 to 90 minutes, leadership in the room)

List every instance of the following for the target account or department. For each: name, owner, frequency, headcount attending or touching, and honest hours per month.

1. Recurring internal meetings (status, standup, WIP, traffic).
2. Recurring client meetings (status calls, check-ins, standing reviews).
3. Review tiers on the content path (who must approve an asset between draft and publish; count the tiers, name the people).
4. Recurring documents produced about work rather than for clients (status decks, WIP trackers, internal recaps).
5. The QBR assembly line: every step from "data export" to "deck in front of client", with hours per step.
6. Approval loops with legal, compliance, or client-side review, and their real median turnaround.

Multiply hours by loaded cost. Write the monthly total on the whiteboard. This number is the reason the room stays engaged for the next section.

## 3. The kill tests

Run each inventory item through the tests in order. First test that fires decides.

| # | Test | If yes | Typical verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Does the step exist only to reassure (a manager, a client, ourselves) that work happened? | KILL. Replace with visibility: gate scores, publish logs, a live dashboard | Most status meetings, most internal recaps |
| K2 | Does the step verify quality in a way a Riverbank gate now verifies earlier? | KILL after the gate is enforcing. Human verification consolidates to ONE pass inside the pod | Review tiers 2 through 4 |
| K3 | Does the step assemble or reformat information that a system can assemble on schedule? | AUTOMATE, then kill the human step. Keep the one hour where a senior turns output into narrative judgment | QBR builds, weekly reports, traffic trackers |
| K4 | Does the step improve the work through judgment that cannot run earlier (taste, strategy, client empathy, regulated review)? | KEEP, and move it as early in the path as it can run (shift left) | Creative direction, counsel review, crisis calls |
| K5 | Does the step train judgment in junior people? | KEEP and protect explicitly; this is the Hourglass intake doing its job (Blueprint 01) | Junior triage of the gate queue beside a senior |

**The QBR worked example.** Typical assembly line: analyst exports (4h), account team drafts narrative (8h), design formats (6h), account director reviews (2h), group director reviews (2h), partner skims (1h), client pre-review call (1h), revisions (4h). Verdicts: K3 kills the export-draft-format chain (auto-assembled readback, C-template gates from Blueprint 03 enforcing data integrity), K2 kills two of the three review tiers, K4 keeps one senior hour turning the readback into decisions, K1 kills the pre-review call in favor of the client seeing the same live readback the pod sees. Twenty-eight hours becomes roughly four, and the four that remain are the only four the client ever valued.

## 4. The kill list document

One page, published internally, no euphemisms. Columns:

| Process | Verdict | Dies on | Replaced by | Hours returned /mo | Owner of the replacement |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Rules for the document:
- Every kill has a date inside 30 days. Undated kills are wishes.
- Every kill names its replacement (a gate, a log, a dashboard, or "nothing, it was pure reassurance").
- The hours-returned column is totaled at the bottom and reported to leadership monthly for one quarter. What gets counted stays dead.

## 5. Running the workshop with a client's leadership (Tier 3 preview)

The same method runs as a half-day executive workshop inside the Transformation Sprint: inventory pre-collected, leadership runs the kill tests live, the session ends with the signed one-page kill list and the 30-day calendar. Agencies who run this on a notoriously slow internal process first (their own QBR line is the classic) walk into the client version with proof instead of promises.

## 6. What not to kill

- Anything a regulator, contract, or counsel requires. The Riverbank routes these earlier; it never deletes them.
- The human community and relationship layer. It is the premium product in decentralized markets, not overhead.
- The junior training loop. It looks like an inefficiency to a spreadsheet; it is your 2030 senior bench.
- Crisis muscle. Standing crisis simulations and escalation trees stay, full stop.

## 7. Narrative audit: the Proof Stack

Once the process kill list is signed, run a second pass over external communications: the Proof Stack audit. This is the narrative equivalent of the Audit to Kill, and it applies the same diagnostic question to claims rather than to meetings. The question: does this statement exist to show something real, or to promise something not yet built?

The Proof Stack framework, drawn from work published by a16z crypto on modern communications strategy, identifies four elements that carry weight with buyers who have been burned by a decade of unfulfilled AI promises. Apply them as a checklist to every active campaign, press release, landing page, and pitch deck.

**Narrative audit checklist**

| # | Test | If it fails | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Partnerships with teeth: does any cited partnership have a publicly verifiable, deployed integration rather than an abstract memorandum of understanding or a logo on a slide? | Strip the partnership claim or replace it with a description of the actual deployed integration. | Partnerships that cannot be verified by an independent journalist hold zero weight; they actively reduce credibility with sophisticated buyers. |
| P2 | Hard data over growth claims: are grandiose descriptors ("rapid growth", "disrupting the category") replaced by specific, verifiable metrics that would withstand independent scrutiny? | Replace the descriptor with the metric, or remove the claim. | Active users, retention curves, and transaction volumes carry weight. Category adjectives do not. |
| P3 | Unmanufactured product-market fit: does the narrative lead with organic, unprompted community or customer behaviour that predates any formal PR push? | Identify actual organic signals and lead with those. If none exist, this section of the narrative is not yet earned. | Authenticity cannot be manufactured through financial incentives or paid placements. Retained active user behaviour is the signal; everything else is decoration. |
| P4 | Third-party validation: is there natural, unsolicited media coverage, independent research, or rigorous audit that an external entity produced without direct compensation or coercion? | Name the third-party source and what it actually said, without implying endorsement of your broader claims. | Unsolicited third-party validation is the highest-trust signal available. Solicited coverage is a distant second and should be labelled accurately. |

**Sequencing rule.** Lead with receipts, vision last. The strongest narrative order is: verified data point, then the organic signal that supports it, then the third-party confirmation, and only then the interpretation of what it means for the road ahead. A vision section that appears before the substance has been established reads as a promise, not a claim. In a market where buyers have been oversold repeatedly, promises are a credibility cost, not a hook.

**Where this fits the Audit to Kill method.** Run the Proof Stack audit immediately after the process kill list is signed. The same hour freed from reassurance meetings is the hour that should go into rebuilding external communications on a substantive foundation. Defragmenting the internal process and then continuing to publish claims that cannot survive scrutiny is a half-measure.
