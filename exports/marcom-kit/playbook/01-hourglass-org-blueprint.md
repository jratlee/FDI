# Blueprint 01: The Hourglass Org Design
### Hourglass vs. Diamond, and how a five-person pod runs an entire campaign

**FDI Agentic MarCom Architecture Kit, Foundation Playbook V1.0. As of 2026.**

---

## 1. The three shapes

**The Pyramid (2019).** Wide junior base doing production, a middle layer managing and reviewing, a narrow senior layer selling and steering. The economics: bill the base out at a multiple, absorb the review layer as overhead. AI broke this shape by making the base's output free.

**The Diamond (the panic response).** Cut the junior base, keep the middle, bulge in the center. It looks efficient on this year's P&L and it is the deskilling trap in org-chart form: the middle layer still reviews at 2019 speed, and there is no intake where the next generation learns taste, client instinct, and media relationships. In 36 months the diamond has senior people with no successors.

**The Hourglass (the durable shape).** Defined once, used throughout this kit: a small top of senior expert generalists executing directly through a shared AI platform, a deliberately lean but protected junior intake at the bottom, and a narrow waist where the old review-management layer used to be. The waist is narrow because verification has shifted left into governance that runs before human review (the Riverbank, Blueprint 03), not because review stopped mattering.

```
   PYRAMID            DIAMOND            HOURGLASS
     /\                  /\                \====/
    /  \                /  \                \  /   senior expert generalists
   /    \              /====\                ||    narrow waist: shifted-left
  /      \             \    /                ||    verification, not tiers
 /________\             \  /                /  \   protected junior intake
                         \/                /____\  (taste pipeline)
```

## 2. Roles in the Hourglass

**Senior expert generalist (top).** A person with one deep specialism (media relations, paid social, analytics, creative) who can now execute competently across the neighboring ones because the platform closes the skill gap. They own client outcomes end to end. Target ratio: one pod of 3 to 5 covers what a 12 to 15 person pyramid team covered.

**Pod lead (top, one per pod).** A senior generalist with the extra job of owning the pod's Riverbank configuration and its kill list (Blueprint 04). Not a people manager in the old sense; there is nobody below them to manage except the intake.

**Junior associate (bottom, protected).** One or two per pod, hired for judgment potential, rotated through every seat. Their job is explicitly NOT production volume (the platform does that). Their job is: sit in the client rooms, run the verification queue with a senior beside them, build the media and community relationships that cannot be automated, and develop taste by comparing model output against senior edits every day. Write this into their role description or the diamond will quietly reassert itself at the next budget review.

**The waist (nearly empty).** Escalation review only: regulated claims, crisis-adjacent content, net-new strategic positions. Everything routine is verified by the Riverbank gates plus one human pass inside the pod.

## 3. Worked example: one pod runs a brand social + GEO program end to end

The scenario: a mid-market consumer brand, monthly retainer covering always-on brand social plus a Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) program (defined once: making the brand retrievable and citable by AI answer engines, the aggregated-market successor to SEO).

**The pod (5 people):**
- Pod lead / strategy generalist (ex-planner)
- Creative generalist (ex-copy lead)
- Channel and community generalist (ex-social manager)
- Analytics and GEO generalist (ex-SEO/analytics)
- Junior associate (rotating seat)

**The weekly cycle:**

| Day | What happens | Who touches it |
|---|---|---|
| Mon AM | Platform generates the week's content slate (40 to 60 assets) from the standing brief plus last week's performance data | Nobody. It runs overnight. |
| Mon PM | Every asset passes the Riverbank gates automatically: tone, claims, disclosure, GEO signal. Roughly a third come back flagged with line-level fixes | Junior associate triages the queue with the creative generalist |
| Tue | One human pass on the survivors; escalation items (new claims, anything touching regulated territory) go to the pod lead and, if needed, client counsel | Creative + channel generalists |
| Wed | Publish and seed. GEO generalist pushes the citable corpus updates (FAQ entries, structured data, source pages) that make the week's story retrievable by answer engines | Channel + analytics generalists |
| Thu | Community layer: the human-judgment work. Replies, creator relationships, escalations. This is deliberately NOT automated | Channel generalist + junior |
| Fri | Performance readback auto-assembles; pod lead spends one hour turning it into the client narrative. The old version of this document took two people two days | Pod lead |

**What was killed to make this possible** (via Blueprint 04): the Monday status call (replaced by the generated slate), two of three review tiers (replaced by the gates), the manual weekly report build (replaced by the auto-readback), and the standing "brand check" meeting with the client (replaced by client visibility into the Riverbank rules themselves, which turns governance into a retention product).

**The economics, directionally.** The pyramid version of this account: 12 to 15 people, half of them below the waterline of the client's perception of value. The Hourglass version: 5 people, all client-visible, with platform costs replacing junior payroll at a fraction of the spread. The margin difference is not from the tools. It is from removing the wrapper the tools made unnecessary. Actual results vary by account mix and how honestly the kill list is executed; treat every number here as a planning shape, not a promise.

## 4. Transition sequencing (one pod, 6 weeks)

1. **Week 1:** Pick the pilot account. Criteria: high-volume production, low regulatory surface, a client relationship that can tolerate visible change.
2. **Week 2:** Run Audit to Kill (Blueprint 04) on that account only. Publish the kill list internally. Expect grief; schedule it.
3. **Week 3:** Install the Riverbank baseline (Blueprint 03) on the account's content workflows. Run it in shadow mode: gates score everything but block nothing.
4. **Week 4:** Staff the pod. Name the junior seat and its protections out loud.
5. **Week 5:** Flip the gates from shadow to enforcing. Kill the meetings on the list. Do both in the same week; the gates are what make the meetings safe to kill.
6. **Week 6:** First full cycle on the new shape. Measure: hours returned, escalation count, client-visible incidents (target: zero), junior verification-queue accuracy.

Do not run this on three accounts at once. The second pod copies a working pattern; the first pod discovers your local failure modes.

## 5. Failure modes to watch

- **The waist regrows.** A senior person starts "just double-checking" the gated output daily. Root cause is usually a miscalibrated gate; fix the rule, not the trust.
- **The junior seat becomes production overflow.** The moment volume spikes, someone will assign the junior to churn output. That is the deskilling trap re-entering through the side door.
- **Shadow mode forever.** Gates that never start enforcing are a dashboard, not a riverbank. Set the flip date in week 3 and keep it.
- **Tomorrow-services drift.** The pod that masters brand social this year should be piloting decentralized community orchestration and agent-callable interfaces next year. The Hourglass is the shape that can absorb those services; the pyramid never could.
