# Time Is Selling Ads to Robots. Good.
### Three business models for the agentic web just showed their cards — and all three price the same scarce asset

Time now sees more bot traffic than human traffic most days. So it started selling ads to the bots.

That's not a joke — it's the first honest business decision of the agentic web. Last month Time began converting its pages to markdown so AI agents could read them more easily; this week, per Digiday's Sara Guaglione, it started serving sponsored FAQ ads inside those pages, with Ally Bank and the Project Management Institute as first buyers. Mobian, Time's ad-tech partner, states the logic plainly: "When you influence ChatGPT, you're influencing potentially all of ChatGPT."

Is Time right to do this? Yes — as a tactic. Bot impressions on authoritative content are scarce, and scarce inventory should be sold at a premium. But watch what Time is *not* claiming: that this is a business model. It's a monetization rail bolted onto something else. And Digiday flags the fragility itself: nobody knows whether LLMs will honor these ads or eventually penalize them as cloaking, the way Google cracked down on ad spam in the 2010s. Time is renting an arbitrage window.

To see the actual business model, you have to look at what the window is cut into. Three builders have now shown three different answers to "how does content get paid on the agentic web" — and they converge somewhere interesting.

**Rail one: sell influence to the agents.** That's Time. The premium rests on domain authority and franchise moments — bot traffic spikes when a Time100 list drops. Note what produces the spike: a human editorial franchise that took decades to build.

**Rail two: meter the door.** Cloudflare's pay-per-crawl, in Matthew Prince's telling to Stratechery, starts from market theory: "all markets require scarcity." Cloudflare's contribution is technical — block the bots by default, then let publishers charge. But look at what the market rewards once the door has a price. Prince wants content scored on two axes: "how reputable is this, and then how novel is it?" His own evidence: Reddit and the New York Times have roughly the same token count, but Reddit extracted seven times the licensing money — because from an LLM's perspective, commodity news is fungible and unique content is not. "It's actually that local, unique, differentiated content, which is the most valuable."

**Rail three: get paid your marginal value.** Parallel — founded by former Twitter CEO Parag Agrawal — computes Shapley values on agent search: what did *your* content uniquely contribute to this answer? Its new Index scores publishers on impressions, citations, value, and uniqueness, and pays out accordingly. Agrawal's prediction for what happens next: creators "will change behavior knowing that there are gaps in the content available to agents... by being unique and differentiated... I can show up and extract disproportionate value."

Three rails, three different owners, three different pricing mechanisms. Now ask: what does each one actually price?

Time's rail prices *authority* — the editorial franchises agents spike for. Cloudflare's rail prices *reputation and novelty* — Prince's two axes. Parallel's rail prices *uniqueness* — the Shapley residual nobody else can supply.

Authority, novelty, uniqueness. Three names for the same input: humans with expertise, compounding over time.

Which is why the fourth voice in this story isn't running a rail at all. New York Times CEO Meredith Kopit Levien, asked by Stratechery why the Times succeeded: "If I can only give one short answer, it is we kept investing in journalism, that's it." Her publisher's formulation: "value and values." Her definition of the product: "human beings with a professional process and real expertise going out into the world and unearthing new information." Every one of the Times' 25,000 recipes is human-tested before it publishes. Humans, she argues, are the moat — and the rails just proved her right, because every rail independently priced her moat as the asset.

Even the rails need the humans. Time's agent ads are drafted by AI but reviewed and approved by people, sold by a human sales team, monitored by humans against the cloaking risk. Prince is recruiting academic economists to design his market's mechanics. Parallel pays for a uniqueness that, so far, only human taste produces at the top of the distribution — and Agrawal expects humans to reorganize their editorial behavior around it.

So here is the sequence for publishers, in order:

1. **Invest in human expertise first.** Not as a value statement — as the only input all three markets price. Franchise editorial, tested recipes, unearthed facts, a beat nobody else covers. If an LLM can get the same thing from five other sources, every rail prices you at commodity.
2. **Make it machine-legible.** Markdown versions, clean structure, citable claims. Time's conversion project is the right move regardless of the ads.
3. **Treat bot traffic as disclosed, monetizable inventory.** Sell the agent ads, meter the crawls, join the marketplaces — plural. The rails are commodity infrastructure; your expertise is not. Plug into all of them.
4. **Measure your AI visibility like you once measured search.** Mobian tracks visibility, favorability, and accuracy of brand answers over time. Parallel's Index shows you what agents think you're worth. These are your new analytics.

But the order matters. Steps 2 through 4 executed on top of commodity content is monetizing your way to zero — better plumbing for water nobody will pay for. Steps 2 through 4 on top of genuine expertise is the compounding machine.

Time's move is right because Time did step 1 for a century first.

The agentic web isn't a threat to expertise-first publishers. It's the first time three independent markets have put a spot price on expertise itself. Build the thing the machines can't make. Then sell it to them on every rail they build.
