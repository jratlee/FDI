## Section 1: The Vibe

### Opening sentence (sensory, market-defining)

In this market, your brand's reputation is partly a matter of public record that anyone can inspect, and the inspectors are not journalists.

### Paragraph 1 (the landscape through a concrete media moment)

In April 2022, the Beanstalk lending protocol lost approximately $182 million in a governance attack. An attacker borrowed enough voting tokens through a flash loan to pass a malicious governance proposal and drain the treasury in a single transaction. The event was visible on the blockchain in real time. There was no spokesperson, no prepared holding statement, no "according to company representatives." The primary source was the ledger itself. Every analyst, reporter, and community member who covered the incident read the same onchain record. What made the reputational consequences severe was not the coverage: it was that the evidence was irrefutable, publicly available, and required no journalist to intermediate it. Any PR professional who has managed a crisis with conflicting accounts, who has negotiated over what the record actually says, was reading a preview of how reputation evidence works in these markets.

### Paragraph 2 (the structural shift, grounded in the research)

Crypto-powered decentralized networks are structurally unlike any market a PR professional has managed before. There is no central feed to win, no single algorithm deciding who gets seen, no platform whose terms of service create the rules of discourse. A brand's presence in a Farcaster channel, a DAO governance forum, or an onchain attestation record is governed by the protocol's own rules, not by a company that can change its mind about access. The Ethereum Name Service has crossed 3.5 million registered names, each one a portable, publicly-resolvable identity that works across every compatible application without a redirect. Sign-In with Ethereum is a standard for asserting wallet identity without giving a platform the relationship. These are not niche developments: they are the early production form of an identity system that, once understood, changes what a PR professional considers "the permanent record."

### Paragraph 3 (what this means for the PR professional, specifically)

For a communications professional, the decentralized market presents both an unfamiliar risk and a genuinely new kind of credibility. The risk: a brand's narrative in decentralized communities is partly written by onchain behavior that the brand cannot edit after the fact. A governance vote, a contribution record, a POAP collection at a community event, these are permanent, public, and verifiable. The brand that participates authentically builds a credibility record that no press release can manufacture. The brand that enters the community extractively, with a promotional announcement and no follow-through, builds an equally permanent record of that. The credibility, in both directions, is the most verifiable form of brand narrative these markets have ever seen. The PR professional who grasps that is ahead of every competitor still treating decentralized communities as a new channel in an old playbook.

## Section 2: What This Means at Your Desk

### Early career (0-5 years)

**The day-to-day reality:** If your agency or client is entering crypto-adjacent communities, you will be doing community monitoring in places that do not work like Twitter or LinkedIn. Farcaster channels, DAO governance forums, and Discord servers are the primary community layers in many decentralized protocols. The content is publicly accessible, but the norms of engagement are different: promotional content is more likely to be called out explicitly, authenticity markers like demonstrable technical understanding and genuine community participation tend to be weighted heavily by community members, and the "record" of a brand's community behavior is more durable and more publicly visible than in traditional social media.

**The skill to build:** Learn to read governance forums and onchain data. You do not need to become a developer. You need to be able to look at a protocol's governance page (most are publicly accessible), understand what the community is currently discussing and voting on, and identify whether the brand's proposed entry or activity is aligned or misaligned with those priorities. A junior PR professional who can do a governance landscape scan before advising on a community activation is doing something most of their peers cannot.

**The habit to start:** When evaluating a new crypto or Web3 client or campaign, look up the primary protocol's governance record before looking at its social channels. The governance record tends to reveal the community's actual priorities more accurately than the marketing site. The PR program that is aligned with those priorities tends to earn community credibility faster than one that is working against them.

### Mid career (5-12 years)

**How this changes program design:** A PR program in decentralized communities is built on contribution cadence rather than campaign cadence. The equivalent of a quarterly media push is a quarter of consistent governance participation, community event hosting, and structured knowledge contribution. The measurement is not clip count; it is participation track record, community standing, and the quality of relationships with recognized protocol contributors, the people whose governance votes carry weight and whose community commentary shapes the narrative.

**How this changes measurement:** Traditional PR measurement tools do not cover decentralized community presence. The relevant metrics include: governance participation rate (how often the brand or its designated participants are voting on relevant proposals), POAP accumulation at community events (an onchain attendance record that any community member can verify), and the quality of engagement with the protocol's recognized contributors. These metrics require different measurement infrastructure than traditional coverage tracking, and building that infrastructure is one of the first practical decisions a mid-career professional advising on a decentralized-market program needs to make.

**How this changes crisis response:** The crisis playbook in decentralized markets leads with the onchain record, not the spokesperson. If a crisis involves an onchain event (a governance failure, a protocol association, a transaction that is publicly visible), the first question is not "what do we say?" but "what does the record show, and is that record accurate?" The PR professional who has been maintaining an authentic participation record is in a fundamentally different crisis position than one who has not, because the record speaks before the statement does.

### Senior and executive (12+ years)

**The structural decision:** The decentralized market is asking senior communications leaders to advise on a form of community investment that has no clear analog in traditional PR budget line items. Governance participation, protocol contribution, and community presence in decentralized networks require sustained, authentic engagement that cannot be outsourced to a content agency running at campaign cadence. The budget decision is: do we treat this as an owned infrastructure investment, staffed for consistency over time, or as a campaign activation, which tends to produce the exactly wrong signal in communities that are specifically attuned to detecting performative brand presence?

**The Hourglass context:** The junior associate in the Hourglass model who is assigned to build and maintain community relationships is doing exactly the right work for decentralized market programs. The relationship-building and community presence that cannot be automated are precisely the activities that tend to matter most in these markets. Protecting the junior seat's capacity for this work is not a nicety; it is the product.

**The conversation with leadership:** The case for decentralized market investment is most credibly framed not as a crypto opportunity but as an early-position infrastructure argument: these networks are the first to ship portable, verifiable identity and programmable settlement as infrastructure, and those properties are going to matter more broadly as agent commerce scales. A brand that understands this market now, when the user base is still early-adopter, is in the same position as a brand that understood search in 2004 or mobile-first in 2011. The window to build a credible onchain presence before the space becomes as competitive as established social channels is relatively early.

## Section 3: Compounding Cohort Decay: The PR/Comms Version

### EL5 explanation

Imagine your brand joins a club. In the beginning, you show up every meeting, you help plan events, and the other club members start to trust you. They know your name. They include you in the conversations that matter. Now imagine you stop showing up for three months. When you come back, the other members are polite, but the trust has faded. The meetings moved on without you. Newer members do not know you. The contribution record in this club is public: anyone can look up who attended which meetings and who voted on which club decisions. Your absence is visible in the record in a way that going quiet on Instagram is not. That is cohort decay in decentralized markets: the trust you built with the community fades when it is not maintained, and the decay is visible to anyone who looks.

### What a "cohort" means in PR/comms terms

In decentralized markets, a cohort is the community members, protocol contributors, and governance participants whose trust and attention the brand has earned during an engagement period. Unlike a traditional PR cohort, which is an audience of readers or viewers who encountered coverage, a decentralized-market cohort is a network of participants whose recognition of the brand is partially documented onchain. Governance votes cast, events attended, contributions made, these are the records that constitute the cohort's relationship with the brand. When those records go stagnant, the cohort does not just forget: it can see that the brand stopped contributing.

### The three decay patterns in the decentralized context

**Coverage Half-Life**

In decentralized markets, "coverage" is distributed across community channels: Farcaster threads, governance forum discussions, protocol newsletters, and the growing ecosystem of crypto-native media. A brand that earns prominent community coverage through a meaningful contribution, a well-received governance proposal, a community event that issues POAPs, tends to see that coverage decay toward the baseline of community memory within two to four weeks, which is faster than in traditional media given the high volume of new content in active protocols. The coverage half-life is lengthened when the brand produces consistent follow-up participation that keeps it in the community's active consideration, and shortened when participation goes quiet.

Cross-reference to aggregated market: In aggregated markets, Coverage Half-Life is primarily influenced by content freshness and AI citation competition. In decentralized markets, it is primarily influenced by participation recency and community activity level. In both cases, the mechanism is the same: a burst of earned presence without an ongoing owned foundation decays faster than one supported by consistent investment.

**Relationship Attrition**

In decentralized communities, the journalist-relationship equivalent is the connection between the brand and the protocol's recognized contributors, the developers, governance delegates, community organizers, and technical voices whose opinions carry weight in the community's deliberation. These relationships are more onchain-transparent than traditional journalist relationships: a contributor's voting record, their protocol history, and their community standing are all publicly visible. A brand that has built strong relationships with recognized contributors during an engagement period and then goes quiet tends to find those relationships attritioning differently than traditional journalist relationships: the contributor can see that the brand has not participated in recent governance, which is a visible signal of disengagement rather than just a quiet period.

Cross-reference to autonomous market: In autonomous markets, Relationship Attrition applies to the relationship between the brand's machine-readable presence and agent retrieval behavior. In decentralized markets, it applies to human community relationships mediated by a public, verifiable participation record.

**Share-of-Voice Erosion**

In decentralized markets, share of voice is measured not in publication mentions but in community presence: how often the brand's name and perspective appear in governance discussions, protocol forums, and community media, relative to competitors who are also active in the space. A brand that enters a decentralized community strongly, generates significant community attention, and then reduces its participation level tends to find that its community share of voice erodes toward zero fairly quickly, because the community's information layer is continuously refreshed by active participants and has limited editorial shelf space for brands that are not currently contributing.

Cross-reference to aggregated market: In aggregated markets, Share-of-Voice Erosion operates at editorial cycle speed and is influenced by coverage activity and owned corpus maintenance. In decentralized markets, it operates at community activity speed and is primarily influenced by participation cadence.

## Section 4: How to Model It: Core Dynamics for the Decentralized Market

> **Disclaimer:** The frameworks below are planning tools for communications professionals, not financial projections. They offer a narrative model for discussing investment tradeoffs with clients and leadership. Nothing in this section is legal, financial, or compliance advice, and nothing constitutes investment guidance related to crypto assets or token-based systems. Actual results vary by organization, market, and execution.

### Module 1: The Participation Record

**EL5 first:** The Participation Record is the onchain and community history of everything your brand has done in the protocol or community: every governance vote, every event attended, every contribution made. It is like a public report card that anyone can read, and it updates in real time. A great Participation Record is the best credential a brand can have in a decentralized community, because it cannot be faked, inflated, or spun. It is simply what the brand has done.

**Professional depth:** The Participation Record is the decentralized market's equivalent of the owned corpus in the aggregated market. It is the durable asset that accumulates with genuine investment and decays with inactivity. For a PR team, the Participation Record is both a planning input (how consistently has the brand been active?) and a measurement output (what does the onchain record show about the brand's community standing?).

What drives it: Consistent governance participation; genuine protocol contribution (code, documentation, integrations); community event hosting with POAP issuance; quality engagement with recognized protocol contributors.

What decays it: Participation gaps that are visible in the public record; promotional announcements without contribution follow-through; activity that is flagged by the community as inauthentic or extractive.

What a PR team can own vs. rent vs. measure: The PR team can own the participation strategy and execution. It cannot rent community credibility. It can measure participation recency, governance vote frequency, POAP issuance and collection, and the quality of contributor relationships through structured community monitoring.

**Worked example:** Q: My client wants to enter the Farcaster community to promote a new product. What should the first ninety days look like? A: The first ninety days should produce no promotional content. Instead, the brand should identify the Farcaster channels most relevant to its category, participate in discussions without a brand agenda for the first thirty days, propose one community contribution (a useful resource, an event, a protocol integration) in the second thirty days, and only introduce the brand's product narrative once it has built enough participation history to be recognized as a genuine community member rather than an advertiser. The promotional announcement that arrives after ninety days of genuine participation tends to land very differently from one that arrives without that foundation.

### Module 2: The Knowledge Provenance System

**EL5 first:** The Knowledge Provenance System is a way of making sure that everything your brand says can be traced back to where it came from. In a decentralized community, people check sources. If your brand makes a claim and someone asks where it came from, and there is no clear answer, the community will notice. If your brand makes a claim and links to a structured, well-sourced page on your own site, and that page has been there for a while and gets updated regularly, the community tends to trust it. The Knowledge Provenance System is the infrastructure that makes your brand's claims checkable.

**Professional depth:** The Knowledge Provenance System is the decentralized market's version of the aggregated market's Owned Corpus. In decentralized communities, the bar for source credibility tends to be higher than in traditional media: community members are accustomed to on-chain verification and tend to apply a similar standard to off-chain claims. A brand that maintains a structured, well-attributed knowledge base, with clear sourcing for each claim, dates on each document, and a consistent update record, is building the Knowledge Provenance System that decentralized-community trust requires.

For a PR team, the Knowledge Provenance System serves double duty: it is the brand's contribution to the community's shared information layer (which builds community credibility) and the journalist briefing infrastructure for any traditional coverage of the brand's decentralized-market activity.

What drives it: Regular publication of structured, sourced knowledge on owned channels; clear attribution for all statistics and technical claims; consistent update schedules with visible revision dates; transparent correction of errors when they occur.

What decays it: Content without clear sourcing; claims that cannot be independently verified; editorial inconsistency (different claims on different pages without explanation).

**Budget modeling narrative (planning framework only, not financial advice):**

The Verification Infrastructure Investment: The cost of building and maintaining a structured, attributed knowledge base that meets decentralized-community sourcing standards. Unlike traditional press kit investment, this is an ongoing editorial cost rather than a campaign cost, because the knowledge base must be continuously updated to remain credible.

The Trust Dividend: The community trust that accumulates when a brand's knowledge claims are consistently verifiable and accurate. In decentralized communities, this trust is partially visible in the quality of community references to the brand's owned content: when recognized contributors cite a brand's knowledge base, they are providing the most valuable form of endorsement available in these markets.

**Worked example:** Q: A community member has publicly disputed a statistic my client used in a Farcaster post. How do I respond? A: The response should be direct, specific, and corrective. In decentralized communities, the worst response to a sourcing challenge is a defensive or vague reply. The best response is to acknowledge the challenge, link to the primary source for the original claim (or, if the source turns out to be weak, to correct the record explicitly). A brand that corrects errors publicly and cites better sources when challenged tends to increase its community credibility through the correction. The community's bar for honest self-correction is lower than its bar for defensive spin.

### Module 3: The Owned Community Layer

**EL5 first:** The Owned Community Layer is the part of the decentralized community that the brand has built and maintains: its Farcaster channel, its governance delegate presence, its event series, its POAP collection program. It is the brand's home in the community, distinct from the protocol's infrastructure, which the brand does not control. Like the owned corpus in the aggregated market, the Owned Community Layer is what the brand can maintain and compound over time, regardless of what any individual protocol does with its own infrastructure.

**Professional depth:** The Owned Community Layer includes: the brand's Farcaster channel and publishing cadence; its governance delegate identity and voting record; its event series and POAP issuance history; and its recognized contributors, the employees or community members who represent the brand in governance discussions and technical forums. Each of these is partially portable: if one protocol changes its terms or declines, the brand's community relationships and participation record may be more durable than its presence on any specific platform.

### Module 4: The Provenance and Traceability Layer

**EL5 first:** In decentralized markets, the Provenance and Traceability Layer is the brand's ability to show its work: where did this claim come from, who made this decision, what was the reasoning? It is like leaving a paper trail that anyone can follow. In a community where trust is built on verifiability rather than authority, the brand that can show its reasoning is more trusted than the one that simply asserts its conclusions.

**Professional depth:** The Provenance and Traceability Layer is the decentralized market's version of the citation and attribution infrastructure in the aggregated market. In PR terms, it is the practice of documenting the reasoning behind brand positions, sourcing claims with primary references, and making governance participation records publicly accessible. For a crisis communications context, the Provenance and Traceability Layer is the brand's first line of defense: a well-documented decision history is the most effective counter-narrative to community criticism based on what the brand "must have known" or "should have done."

## Section 5: Common Questions Answered

**Q1: My client wants to do an airdrop. Is that a PR strategy?**

An airdrop is a community formation event, and it tends to function as a PR event whether designed that way or not. The most effective airdrops, like Uniswap's UNI distribution and ENS's governance token launch, are cited in crypto communities as genuine community-formation successes because they rewarded historical participants rather than prospective speculators, and because they were structured around contribution (past usage) rather than demographic targeting. A poorly designed airdrop, one that produces a short-term price spike and a long-term community of participants with no genuine affiliation, tends to generate coverage in the opposite direction. The PR team's role in an airdrop is to help ensure the eligibility criteria and the community narrative are aligned: the brand should be able to explain, in plain language, why the recipients were chosen and what it hopes the airdrop will build.

**Q2: How do I explain decentralized social media to a client who has never heard of Farcaster?**

The most accessible frame is the pre-algorithm web analogy: Farcaster is more like the early web than like current social media. It is a protocol that anyone can build on, rather than a platform that controls the feed and sells advertising against it. A brand that publishes on Farcaster owns its content in the sense that no single company can remove it based on a policy change. The audience is smaller than Twitter, but the community tends to be technically credentialed, crypto-literate, and engaged at a higher per-capita rate than large platform audiences. The PR value is not reach; it is community credibility with an audience that tends to be influential in the crypto space. If the client's target audience includes crypto-native developers, investors, or early adopters, Farcaster presence tends to be a more relevant signal than presence on any major social platform.

**Q3: A competitor has been active in a DAO's governance and we have not. How significant is that gap?**

The gap tends to matter more than its direct PR impact suggests, because governance participation in decentralized communities generates compound effects. A competitor that has been consistently voting, proposing, and engaging in governance discussions has built a governance record that functions as a form of community tenure. Community members who have interacted with that record over time have a more formed opinion of the competitor's priorities and reliability than they have of a brand that has not participated. Closing the gap requires time and consistent participation, not a campaign burst. The most effective response is to identify the governance areas where the brand has a genuine perspective, begin participating in those areas specifically, and be transparent about the fact that the brand is new to active participation. Trying to immediately match a competitor's governance volume without a genuine perspective tends to be more visible and more damaging than a measured, authentic entry.

**Q4: How do I explain to a reporter that a crypto community is a legitimate media channel?**

The most persuasive frame for a reporter is the audience quality argument rather than the reach argument. Farcaster and Lens communities include significant concentrations of crypto-native developers, investors, protocol founders, and technical journalists. Coverage in a major crypto-native publication or prominent community discussion thread tends to reach an audience that is disproportionately influential in the crypto ecosystem, even if the raw audience numbers are smaller than a major consumer outlet. The secondary argument is the verifiability argument: coverage and community discussion in these channels often includes onchain verification that mainstream media does not provide, which can be a useful authentication mechanism for journalists covering the space.

**Q5: My client made a governance decision that the community is unhappy with. What does the crisis response look like?**

The crisis response in a decentralized community leads with the record, not the statement. Before drafting any public response, the first step is to review the onchain record of the governance decision: what was proposed, how was it voted on, what was the reasoning documented in the governance forum? The public response should acknowledge the community's concern directly, reference the governance record, explain the reasoning behind the decision in plain language, and, where relevant, commit to a specific governance action as a follow-up (a proposal to revisit the decision, a community call to discuss the concerns). The brand that responds to community criticism by engaging the governance process tends to recover more quickly than one that issues a traditional holding statement and waits for the news cycle to pass.

**Q6: What does "verifiable onchain identity" actually mean for a PR team in practice?**

In practice, it means the brand has a registered ENS name (brand.eth or equivalent), has used Sign-In with Ethereum to authenticate its presence in Farcaster and other protocol applications, and has a wallet address with a visible history of community participation. The PR implication is that the brand's identity in these communities is independently verifiable without the brand's involvement: any community member can resolve the ENS name, inspect the wallet's onchain history, and form a view of the brand's authenticity without consulting the press room. This is a form of radical transparency that has no traditional PR equivalent, and it tends to reward brands that have been genuinely active and penalize brands that have been performative.

**Q7: Should every PR client have a decentralized market strategy?**

No. The decentralized market is most relevant for brands operating in or adjacent to crypto, DeFi, Web3, and the developer tools ecosystem. For brands whose audiences are not present in crypto-native communities, the investment may not justify the return in the near term. The more forward-looking argument, and one appropriate for clients with a three-to-five year planning horizon, is that the identity and settlement infrastructure being built in crypto-native networks is likely to have broader application as agent commerce scales. Understanding these markets now, and building a foundational presence, tends to be more efficient than entering after the space has become as competitive as established social platforms. Whether that argument applies to a specific client depends on their audience, their planning horizon, and their tolerance for early-stage community investment.

## Section 6: Takeaways

**Takeaway 1:** Community credibility in decentralized markets is an asset you build, not a position you buy.

The brand that enters a decentralized community with a promotional announcement and no contribution history is demonstrating exactly the behavior these communities are designed to resist. The brand that spends ninety days as a genuine participant before introducing its own narrative is building a participation record that is publicly verifiable and compounding in community credibility. Shift the first quarter's budget from campaign production to community presence, and be specific about what "presence" means: governance votes, forum contributions, event hosting, direct engagement with recognized contributors.

**Takeaway 2:** The governance record is the brand narrative.

In traditional PR, the press release is the brand's controlled statement. In decentralized markets, the governance record is equally or more authoritative: it shows, rather than says, what the brand values and how it behaves when its interests and the community's interests are in tension. A brand with a consistent, well-reasoned governance voting history has a form of brand narrative that no communications team can produce through drafting alone. Assign someone on the team to own governance monitoring and participation the way you would assign someone to own the media calendar, with accountable output and a visible schedule.

**Takeaway 3:** Stop treating the knowledge base as a support function. Start treating it as a community contribution.

In decentralized communities, a brand that publishes structured, well-sourced, accurate knowledge about its area of expertise is making a contribution to the community's shared information layer. That contribution tends to be more valued and more durable than promotional content, because it serves the community's needs rather than the brand's announcement calendar. The investment in a structured, attributable knowledge base is simultaneously a community credibility asset, an AI-citability asset in the aggregated market, and a journalist briefing infrastructure for traditional coverage. It is the single investment with the broadest return across all three market types.
