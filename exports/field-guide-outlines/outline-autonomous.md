## Section 1: The Vibe

### Opening sentence (sensory, market-defining)

Your next customer may never see your press room, your pitch, or your carefully placed feature story, because the entity buying on their behalf does not read.

### Paragraph 1 (the landscape through a concrete market moment)

On September 29, 2025, Stripe and OpenAI launched Instant Checkout inside ChatGPT. Users with more than 700 million weekly sessions could buy products from Etsy sellers and, rolling out, over a million Shopify merchants without leaving the chat interface. The Agentic Commerce Protocol underlying that launch was published under Apache 2.0: an open specification for making a checkout agent-ready. Two weeks before that, Google announced its own protocol, Agent Payments Protocol, with more than sixty partners including Mastercard, American Express, PayPal, and Coinbase. These were not research announcements. They were production deployments backed by the financial infrastructure incumbents. Morgan Stanley reported in December 2025 that roughly 23% of Americans had already made a purchase using AI in the prior month. Adobe, measuring more than a trillion visits to US retail sites, found AI-referred traffic up 393% year over year in the first quarter of 2026. When the payment protocols, the major AI platforms, and the financial measurement companies are all pointing at the same quarter, the professional response is not to wait and see.

### Paragraph 2 (the PR consequence, stated directly)

The coverage your program earns, the story placed in a business publication, the analyst brief, the CEO profile, is designed to reach a human reader who makes a judgment about the brand and eventually makes a decision. In autonomous markets, the buyer is increasingly a program that receives a goal, evaluates sources, and completes a transaction within preset limits, without reading a story or forming a brand opinion. That program's evaluation criteria are not narrative quality or spokesperson credibility. They are machine-readability, source verifiability, structured consistency, and the presence of the brand's information in trusted third-party references. Adobe found that major portions of US retail websites are not entirely readable by machines, which limits their visibility across AI search results. The brands with excellent traditional PR programs and non-machine-readable owned content are, in the autonomous market's terms, invisible. The gap is real, it is measurable, and it is not closed by publishing more traditional coverage.

### Paragraph 3 (the opportunity, specifically for communications professionals)

The communications professional who understands this is not in a worse position than before. They are in a better position than most of their clients' other advisors, because the properties that make a brand's information trustworthy to an agent overlap substantially with the properties that good PR has always pursued: named, attributed claims; specific, verifiable statistics; consistent identity across all published surfaces; and a track record of accurate, sourced, professionally maintained information. GEO (Generative Engine Optimization) is the discipline of making content citable by AI answer engines, and it is the natural extension of what a PR professional already does when briefing a journalist with structured, attributable data. The autonomous market does not require the communications professional to become a developer. It requires them to understand that the audience now includes machines, and to structure the brand's information accordingly.

## Section 2: What This Means at Your Desk

### Early career (0-5 years)

**The day-to-day reality:** The assets you produce, press releases, executive Q&As, media briefing documents, are going to be read by machines as well as humans in an increasing share of contexts. The machine's reading comprehension is different from a human's: it looks for consistent structure, named sources, attributed statistics, and sequential organization. A beautifully written press release that is light on data, heavy on narrative, and structured for scanning by a busy editor is a less valuable asset in autonomous markets than a moderately written document with specific, attributed claims organized under consistent headings with a clear update date.

**The skill to build:** Practice what might be called "agent-readable drafting": reading your own work through the filter of what an AI retrieval system would extract from it. Which claims are named and attributed? Which are general assertions without a source? Which statistics have a clear provenance, and which are "industry believes" constructions that an agent cannot verify? This is not a new editorial standard; it is the editorial standard your editors and fact-checkers already apply, made explicit and applied at the drafting stage rather than the review stage.

**The habit to start:** When you submit a draft, add a one-paragraph "machine-readability note" at the top identifying the three most citable claims in the piece: the claim with the strongest attributed source, the statistic with the clearest provenance, and the executive quote that is most likely to be cited accurately by an AI system. This is not a formal deliverable; it is a habit that builds the editorial judgment the autonomous market will continue to reward.

### Mid career (5-12 years)

**How this changes program design:** The autonomous market does not replace the traditional earned media program. It adds a layer beneath it: the machine-readable owned infrastructure that determines whether the coverage the program earns has a durable foundation for agent retrieval. A PR program designed only at the coverage layer, without attention to the owned infrastructure that agents query, is building a reputation in a medium that an increasing share of the discovery stack does not read. The program that addresses both layers tends to produce coverage that is more durable and more citable than coverage alone.

**How this changes measurement:** The emerging measurement category is agent retrieval frequency: how often a brand appears in agent-generated answers about its category, and how that frequency compares to competitors. This is harder to measure than traditional clip count, but the tooling is developing quickly. Adding one structured agent-query audit per quarter, running a set of defined category queries through major AI assistants and recording brand presence versus competitors, tends to be the practical starting point for mid-career professionals advising clients on autonomous market readiness.

**How this changes pitching:** A media pitch that results in a story with specific, attributed statistics, named sources, and a direct link to a structured brand knowledge page tends to generate more durable agent-citability than a narrative-only placement. This is not a reason to make every pitch feel like a technical briefing. It is a reason to ensure that every pitch is supported by attributable evidence that the resulting story can reference, so that when an agent retrieves the story, the brand's claims survive the retrieval with their accuracy intact.

### Senior and executive (12+ years)

**The structural decision:** The autonomous market is asking senior communications leaders to make an infrastructure investment that has no clear precedent in traditional PR budgeting. The machine-readable owned presence, the structured knowledge base, the consistent identity across all brand surfaces, is not a campaign cost or a retainer cost. It is closer to an infrastructure cost: an ongoing investment in the substrate that makes all other communications investment more durable. The budget decision is whether to make that investment as a first-mover, before the space becomes competitive, or to wait until the cost of recovery from a machine-readability deficit is higher than the cost of proactive investment. Evidence suggests early movers tend to build more durable positions; the specific timing and investment level depend on client situation and category dynamics.

**The security risk conversation:** The autonomous market introduces a category of reputational risk that has no traditional PR equivalent: trust-boundary failure in an agent-callable system. If a brand's owned content contains elements that trigger agent safety filters, whether because of ambiguous claims, unverified statistics, or structural properties that look like potential injection vectors, the brand may be excluded from agent citations entirely rather than ranked lower. This is a binary failure mode, and recovering from it tends to require structural changes rather than coverage activity. Pre-crisis planning for autonomous market reputation risks is a service category that communications professionals are well-positioned to develop for clients who are building agent-facing interfaces.

**The conversation with leadership:** The case for autonomous market investment is most credibly framed as a machine-readability gap closure, not an AI trend response. "Our owned content may not be readable by the agents that are increasingly mediating our customers' discovery and purchase decisions. Adobe has measured this gap across major US retail sites. Here is what our current owned content looks like against the machine-readability criteria, here is the gap relative to competitors who have closed it, and here is the investment required to close it over the next two quarters." That is an infrastructure conversation, not a technology conversation.

## Section 3: Compounding Cohort Decay: The PR/Comms Version

### EL5 explanation

Imagine you have a really good reference book about your neighborhood. It was written two years ago and it is very accurate. But now there are newer, more detailed books about the same neighborhood that get updated every month. When someone's computer assistant tries to find information about the neighborhood, it tends to use the newer, more updated books rather than yours, even though yours was excellent when it was written. Your book still exists. It is still accurate. But the assistant's memory of which books are current and trustworthy has shifted toward the ones that keep getting better. That is compounding cohort decay in autonomous markets: the audience the machine builds for your brand slowly migrates toward brands that keep their machine-readable information current, and away from brands that published excellent content once and then moved on.

### What a "cohort" means in PR/comms terms

In autonomous markets, a cohort is the set of agent retrieval events that reference a brand during a defined period: the queries where the brand appeared in AI-generated answers, the agent-mediated purchase decisions where the brand was in the consideration set, and the third-party citations that corroborated the brand's claims during that period. Unlike a traditional audience cohort, which consists of human readers with memories and relationships, the autonomous market cohort is constituted by algorithmic references that decay when fresher, better-structured sources appear. The PR program that treats this cohort as a fixed asset, rather than one that requires ongoing investment to maintain, tends to find it eroding faster than traditional measurement reflects.

### The three decay patterns in the autonomous context

**Coverage Half-Life**

In autonomous markets, the half-life of content that an agent will cite tends to be shorter than in human-mediated discovery, because agents weight recency more heavily and have access to a much larger pool of competing sources. A research report published eight months ago may be an authoritative source for a human reader who knows its provenance. An agent evaluating current sources for a recommendation tends to weight it against fresher alternatives from competitors who have published more recently. The half-life is extended by regular updates to the owned content (AirOps research suggests pages not updated quarterly are approximately three times more likely to lose citations), by the presence of the content in third-party corroboration, and by the machine-readability quality of the owned page.

Cross-reference to aggregated market: Coverage Half-Life in aggregated markets is influenced by content aging and competitor corpus growth. In autonomous markets, it is accelerated by agent retrieval's heavier weighting of recency, making the update cadence investment more urgent.

**Relationship Attrition**

In autonomous markets, Relationship Attrition applies not to journalist relationships but to the relationship between the brand's machine-readable presence and agent retrieval systems' evaluation of it. As agent systems update their retrieval behavior based on freshness, citation corroboration, and structural quality, a brand whose owned content has not been updated or whose third-party corroboration has not grown tends to find its retrieval frequency declining. This is relationship attrition at algorithm speed: there is no quarterly check-in to reset the relationship. The relationship is continuously re-evaluated against the current state of the brand's machine-readable presence.

Cross-reference to decentralized market: In decentralized markets, Relationship Attrition is mediated by a public participation record that community members can inspect. In autonomous markets, it is mediated by agent retrieval algorithms that weight current evidence. Both forms of attrition are accelerated by inactivity and mitigated by consistent, structured investment.

**Share-of-Voice Erosion**

In autonomous markets, Share-of-Voice Erosion operates at algorithm speed and tends to be less visible than in traditional or decentralized markets: a brand does not receive a community notification that its agent retrieval frequency has declined. The erosion tends to become visible only when the brand tracks agent-query presence systematically over time, which most brands are not yet doing. The brands that are tracking this metric tend to find that competitors who have made machine-readability investments are capturing a growing share of the agent-retrieval consideration set even if their traditional coverage volume is comparable.

Cross-reference to aggregated market: In aggregated markets, Share-of-Voice Erosion is measurable through AI share of voice monitoring tools. In autonomous markets, the equivalent measurement requires systematic agent-query testing across the brand's category, which is less tooled than AI share of voice but follows the same logic.

## Section 4: How to Model It: Core Dynamics for the Autonomous Market

> **Disclaimer:** The frameworks below are planning tools for communications professionals, not financial projections. They offer a narrative model for discussing investment tradeoffs with clients and leadership. Nothing in this section is legal, financial, or compliance advice. Actual results vary by organization, market, and execution.

### Module 1: The Machine-Legible Interface

**EL5 first:** The Machine-Legible Interface is everything your brand has published that a computer program can find, read, understand, and repeat accurately without needing a human to explain it. Think of it as the brand's translation into a language that agents speak. If the brand's press room is machine-legible, an agent researching the brand's category can read it, pull the relevant claims, and cite them accurately. If it is not machine-legible, the agent will cite someone else's version of your brand's story, or leave the brand out of the answer entirely.

**Professional depth:** The Machine-Legible Interface is the autonomous market's primary owned asset. It includes the brand's press room and newsroom (structured for machine retrieval), the executive commentary archive (with consistent headings and attribution), the FAQ and knowledge base (organized around the specific questions agents are asked about the brand's category), and the schema markup and structured data that make the underlying content interpretable by retrieval systems. For a PR team, this is editorial work with a technical layer: the content must be good journalism and good machine-readable structure simultaneously.

What drives it: Regular owned content publication with GEO-optimized structure; schema markup on key pages; consistent brand terminology across all owned surfaces; fresh publication dates and visible update records; third-party corroboration through earned mentions and citations.

What decays it: Content aging without updates; structural inconsistency (different formatting on different pages); claims that cannot be independently verified; brand terminology that varies across surfaces (making it harder for agents to resolve a consistent identity).

What a PR team can own vs. rent vs. measure: The PR team owns the editorial quality and update schedule of the Machine-Legible Interface. It cannot rent agent-citability; it must be earned through structural quality and third-party corroboration. It can measure citation persistence, agent-query presence, and owned-content structural quality through GEO audits and emerging agent-monitoring tools.

**Budget modeling narrative (planning framework only, not financial advice):**

The Machine-Readability Gap Cost: The opportunity cost of being excluded from agent-mediated discovery due to non-machine-readable owned content. Adobe's finding that major portions of US retail websites are not fully machine-readable suggests this gap is common rather than exceptional, which means closing it before competitors do tends to generate a first-mover advantage in agent-mediated consideration.

The Compounding Return on Legibility Investment: A Machine-Legible Interface that is maintained with consistent updates and good editorial structure tends to accumulate agent-citability over time as the brand's content builds a citation history. Unlike a campaign's coverage, the Machine-Legible Interface is an asset the brand controls and can improve continuously.

**Worked example:** Q: My client's CEO gave a major keynote and earned 200 clips. In the autonomous market, what do I do next? A: Within two weeks of the keynote, publish a structured summary of the CEO's key claims on the owned newsroom, with attributed statistics, consistent headings, schema markup, and a clear publication date. This creates a machine-readable anchor for the keynote's content that agents can retrieve and cite independently of the third-party coverage. The coverage will appear in agent answers about the industry event; the owned summary will appear in agent answers about the brand's specific positions and expertise. Both are valuable, and they serve different retrieval contexts.

### Module 2: The Citation Authority Stack

**EL5 first:** The Citation Authority Stack is the combination of the brand's own content plus all the outside sources that mention or reference it. Think of it like a reputation built from two sides: what you say about yourself (your owned content), and what trusted others say about you (your third-party references). Agents trust brands more when both sides are strong. A brand with excellent owned content but no third-party corroboration tends to be less trusted by agents than one with good owned content and substantial third-party mentions. The stack compounds: more good owned content tends to generate more third-party references, which tends to generate more agent trust, which tends to generate more mentions.

**Professional depth:** The Citation Authority Stack is the autonomous market's version of the domain authority concept from search engine optimization, extended to agent retrieval. It includes: the brand's owned corpus quality and freshness (the foundation), its earned coverage and third-party mention volume (the corroboration layer), and its presence in trusted authoritative sources such as Wikipedia, industry publications, and academic references (the authority signal layer). Research from Ahrefs found that web mentions correlated with AI visibility far more strongly than backlinks, which means the PR team's earned coverage work is directly contributing to the Citation Authority Stack alongside the owned content investment.

**Worked example:** Q: Our brand appears in AI answers about our category, but always as a secondary mention after two competitors. What is driving that, and what can we do? A: Secondary mention positioning tends to be driven by Citation Authority Stack gaps: either the owned content is less structured and fresh than competitors', or the third-party corroboration is thinner, or both. The practical diagnostic is a three-part audit: compare the freshness and structure of the brand's owned category content against the two competitors' equivalent pages; compare the volume and quality of third-party mentions (particularly in authoritative publications) across the three brands; and run an AI share of voice test across ten to fifteen key category queries to map exactly where the secondary positioning is occurring. The audit tends to identify a prioritized list of gaps, with owned content freshness and structure typically being the most actionable near-term lever.

### Module 3: The Trust Boundary

**EL5 first:** The Trust Boundary is the fence an agent puts around what it is willing to repeat. Agents are designed to be careful about information they retrieve: they tend to prefer information that comes from named, verifiable sources, is internally consistent, is not making extreme or unverifiable claims, and is corroborated by other trusted sources. A brand whose content is well-organized, accurately sourced, and internally consistent tends to pass the Trust Boundary test. A brand whose content contains unverified claims, inconsistent terminology, or extreme language tends to be treated more cautiously, which may mean being cited less frequently or not at all in agent answers.

**Professional depth:** The Trust Boundary is the autonomous market's expression of the security principle: treat every retrieved document as hostile input, and sanitize everything that goes out. For a brand, the Trust Boundary has a PR translation: the content that agents retrieve about the brand will be repeated, at machine speed and machine scale, to the brand's potential customers. Content that is inaccurate, inconsistent, or structurally ambiguous will be repeated inaccurately. Content that is well-structured, attributed, and internally consistent will be repeated accurately. The Trust Boundary framework is not a security issue for most PR teams; it is an editorial quality issue with autonomous-market consequences.

**The trust-boundary failure mode:** A brand whose content triggers agent safety filters, through ambiguous claims, unverifiable statistics, or content that resembles potential injection vectors, may be excluded from agent citations entirely rather than ranked lower. This is a binary failure mode with no traditional PR equivalent, and recovery tends to require structural editorial changes rather than coverage activity. The most effective mitigation is the same editorial practice that good PR has always pursued: accurate, attributed, verifiable, internally consistent content.

### Module 4: The Agentic Discovery Surface

**EL5 first:** The Agentic Discovery Surface is everything an agent can find about the brand when it is looking: the owned content, the third-party references, the API surface if the brand has one, and the brand's presence in any agent-readable directories or registries. It is the sum of all the ways an agent can discover the brand exists and evaluate whether it is worth citing or transacting with. A brand with a large, well-maintained Agentic Discovery Surface is visible to agents across a wide range of queries. A brand with a small or poorly-maintained surface tends to appear in fewer agent answers and fewer agent-mediated consideration sets.

**Professional depth:** The Agentic Discovery Surface extends the aggregated market's Citation Surface concept to include agent-specific discovery mechanisms: MCP server registration (for brands with agent-callable interfaces), Agent Card presence (for brands operating in the A2A protocol layer), and ACP/AP2 compatibility (for brands in agent-mediated commerce). For most PR teams, the practical components of the Agentic Discovery Surface are the owned content layer and the third-party corroboration layer, which are within the team's direct influence. The more technical components, MCP servers and protocol registration, are engineering decisions that the PR team should understand and monitor without necessarily owning.

## Section 5: Common Questions Answered

**Q1: What do I tell my client when they say "our customers are not using AI to buy our product yet"?**

The most useful response is to separate the question of current behavior from the question of current investment. Adobe found that AI-referred traffic converts to purchase at a higher rate than non-AI traffic, which suggests that the customers who are using AI to research purchases may be disproportionately valuable customers. Morgan Stanley found that roughly 23% of Americans had made a purchase using AI in the prior month, and that number is likely to grow as the checkout integrations mature. The question is not whether to wait for the customer behavior to shift; it is whether to be positioned for that shift before or after competitors have built their machine-readable presence. The cost of building a structured, agent-citable owned presence before the behavior shift tends to be lower than the cost of recovering from a machine-readability gap after competitors have taken the early positions.

**Q2: How do I explain MCP to a client who has no technical background?**

MCP (Model Context Protocol) is a standard for making a brand's knowledge and services accessible to AI agents in the same way that a wire service makes a brand's press releases accessible to news organizations. Just as a brand that publishes on PR Newswire makes its announcements available to every subscriber without choosing which outlets receive it, a brand that publishes an MCP-compatible interface makes its knowledge available to every AI agent that supports the standard. The brand does not control which agents query it; the infrastructure makes it available to all of them. For clients who have not yet built an MCP interface, the practical near-term implication is to ensure the owned content layer, the press room and knowledge base, is as machine-readable as possible so that agents querying the brand through general web retrieval get the same quality of information they would get from a structured MCP interface.

**Q3: My client's product is purchased through a physical retailer, not online. Does the autonomous market apply to them?**

Yes, through two mechanisms. First, AI agents are increasingly used for research and consideration, even when the ultimate purchase happens in a physical location. An agent helping a user decide which retailer to visit for a specific product category is making a recommendation that influences the physical purchase. A brand that is well-cited in agent answers about its category may benefit in physical retail in ways that do not show up in direct e-commerce attribution. Second, the autonomous market's influence on B2B purchasing, where procurement agents are beginning to evaluate and shortlist vendors, tends to be relevant for brands with any B2B dimension even if their consumer distribution is primarily physical.

**Q4: An AI assistant is giving inaccurate information about my client's brand. What is the PR response?**

The PR response to AI-generated inaccuracies has two layers. The immediate layer is the same as any inaccuracy correction: identify the most likely sources that the AI is drawing from (Wikipedia, major publications, the brand's own owned content), correct any errors in those sources, and ensure the brand's owned content has accurate, clearly attributed information. The AI system's outputs tend to update as the sources it references are updated, though the timing is unpredictable. The structural layer is to audit the brand's owned content for ambiguities or outdated information that could be the source of the inaccuracy, and to implement an update schedule that keeps the owned content current. The most common source of AI-generated inaccuracies about a brand is outdated owned content or Wikipedia entries that have not been maintained.

**Q5: How do I explain the trust-boundary security concept in client-facing language?**

The clearest client-facing translation is: agents are careful about what they repeat. An agent that encounters content with unverified claims, inconsistent terminology, or structural ambiguities tends to treat that content more cautiously than content that is clearly attributed, internally consistent, and corroborated by external sources. In practice, this means the editorial standards that good PR has always pursued, accuracy, attribution, internal consistency, are exactly the standards that make content agent-trustworthy. There is no new standard to meet; there is a new consequence if the existing standard is not met. A brand whose content does not pass the agent's quality check may be excluded from agent citations entirely, which is a qualitatively different consequence from low search ranking. The mitigation is the same editorial investment that good PR has always required.

**Q6: My client is worried about competitors using AI agents to scrape or misrepresent their brand information. Is that a real risk?**

Indirect prompt injection, where content retrieved by an agent contains instructions that alter the agent's behavior, is a documented security vulnerability that OWASP ranks as the top risk for LLM applications. In a brand context, a sophisticated actor who embeds misleading claims in content that agents retrieve about a competitor could, in theory, influence agent-generated answers about that competitor. This is an emerging risk area with no established PR playbook. The most effective mitigation the PR team can pursue is ensuring the brand's owned content is the most authoritative, well-structured, and widely-corroborated source about the brand available, so that it tends to outweigh lower-quality competing signals in agent retrieval. This is the same "build the strongest corpus" investment that addresses the coverage half-life and share-of-voice challenges in the aggregated market.

**Q7: Should we be building an MCP server for our client?**

For most PR clients, the answer in the near term is: ensure the owned content layer is machine-readable before investing in an MCP server. An MCP server is the agent-callable interface layer, and it tends to be most valuable for brands that already have a well-structured, machine-readable knowledge base to expose through it. A brand with a poorly-maintained, non-machine-readable press room and knowledge base that builds an MCP server has put the agent-callable interface on top of a foundation that agents will not trust. The sequencing tends to be: audit and improve the owned content layer first, measure the resulting improvement in agent-query presence, and then evaluate whether an MCP server investment would generate additional discoverability that the improved owned content alone cannot produce.

## Section 6: Takeaways

**Takeaway 1:** The next media relations skill is agent-readable drafting.

Every piece of content the PR team produces, from a press release to an executive Q&A to a media briefing document, now has an agent audience alongside its human audience. Audit the last five press releases your team produced. Count the attributed statistics, the named sources, and the claims that are verifiable from a linked primary source. The ones that score well on those criteria are already agent-ready. The ones that do not tend to be heavier on narrative and lighter on structure. Building the habit of agent-readable drafting before the review stage is a skill that tends to improve the quality of human-facing content at the same time it improves agent-citability.

**Takeaway 2:** Stop treating the knowledge base as a support cost. It is the PR program's most agent-critical asset.

Adobe's measurement found that a significant share of US retail websites are structurally invisible to machine retrieval. For a PR program that generates excellent coverage but maintains an outdated, non-machine-readable owned knowledge base, that coverage is being built on a foundation that agents are increasingly unable to access. The knowledge base update schedule deserves the same budget priority as the media pitch calendar: it is the substrate that determines whether the coverage compounds in agent-citability or decays to the coverage-without-corpus baseline.

**Takeaway 3:** Add one agent-query audit per quarter to the standard measurement readout.

Before the autonomous market was measurable, the case for machine-readability investment was theoretical. It is now measurable with basic tools: run ten to fifteen key category queries through major AI assistants, record which brands appear and in what context, compare to the previous quarter, and compare to two to three competitors. That audit, done consistently, tends to give the PR program a leading indicator of where agent-mediated share of voice is shifting before it shows up in traditional coverage metrics. It also tends to give the CFO conversation about machine-readability investment a baseline and a trend line, which is more persuasive than a structural argument alone.
