## Section 1: The Vibe

### Opening sentence (sensory, market-defining)

The click your coverage used to earn is now being absorbed somewhere your measurement dashboard cannot see.

### Paragraph 1 (the landscape through a concrete media moment)

In February 2025, Chegg filed suit against Google and stated in plain terms that its business "depends on referrals from Google's monopoly search engine for a large portion of the revenue." Chegg did not win a legal victory; it made a public record. Its stock fell to approximately $1. The case is not about Chegg specifically. It is about what happens when a company's discovery model runs entirely through an intermediary it does not control, and that intermediary changes the rules. Seven months later, Penske Media, parent of Rolling Stone, Variety, and Billboard, became the first major U.S. news publisher to sue Google over AI Overviews, alleging a "coercive bargain": allow your content into AI summaries or accept worse search rankings. Two companies, representing education and entertainment media, arrived at the same moment by the same mechanism. The PR professional who reads those filings and thinks "that's a tech company problem" is reading the wrong subject line.

### Paragraph 2 (the behavioral evidence, grounded)

Pew Research observed the actual browsing behavior of 900 U.S. adults across nearly 69,000 Google searches. When an AI-generated summary appeared, users were less likely to click any link. Roughly 1% clicked a source cited inside the summary. Bain found that about 60% of searches now end without the user visiting any website, and that AI-generated summaries are now part of at least 40% of searches for roughly 80% of users. Global publisher traffic from Google fell an estimated third in the year to November 2025. These are not predictions about where things are heading. They are measurements of what is already happening. The traditional model of PR, place coverage, drive traffic, demonstrate reach, has a new leak in it that most measurement systems do not yet show.

### Paragraph 3 (what this means for the PR professional, specifically)

The clip exists. The visit may not. That gap is the aggregated market's central PR challenge, and it is also its central opportunity. A brand whose communications program has been building a structured, verified, citable owned corpus, the PR newsroom that is actually updated, the research that is attributed and machine-readable, the executive commentary that has consistent headings and sourcing, is already doing most of what the aggregated market rewards. The brands that recognize this and instrument it, that start measuring AI share of voice alongside traditional clip count, that structure their next pitch with the same care they give a wire release, will find that earned media has a longer shelf life than it used to. The brands that do not will find that an excellent quarter of coverage fades faster than it should, because the AI answer layer summarized it and moved on.

**Visual anchor 1: The Click Gap diagram.** A simple split-bar chart showing the old model (search leads to click, click leads to visit, visit leads to conversion) against the new model (search leads to AI summary, AI summary absorbs the query, 1% of users click the cited source). The gap between "coverage exists" and "visit happens" is labeled "the absorption zone." This makes the problem tangible without requiring technical explanation.

The brand's own domain is shown as a small dot far from the concentration zone. The caption asks: "Where is your brand's owned content on this map?" ## Section 2: What This Means at Your Desk

### Early career (0-5 years)

**The day-to-day reality:** The metrics you are being asked to track, clip count, estimated reach, AVE, are the outputs of a measurement system that was designed for a world where the clip reliably produced a visit. In the aggregated market, that assumption is loosening. You may be producing accurate clip counts for a campaign that is generating less actual audience contact than the numbers suggest.

**The skill to build:** Learn to audit the machine-readability of owned content. This means understanding what makes a press release, a news post, or an executive Q&A citable by an AI system rather than just readable by a human. It is not a technical skill. It is an editorial skill: clear headings, attributed statistics, named quotes with context, fresh publication dates, consistent brand terminology. The PR professional who can look at a draft and identify which elements will survive an AI summarization, and which will get lost, has a capability that is genuinely new and genuinely valuable. Start practicing it on everything you edit.

**The habit to start:** When a major piece of coverage runs, check whether the brand appears in AI answers about the story's topic. Not to replace traditional measurement, but to start building intuition for the relationship between coverage and AI presence. That intuition will be the foundation of a measurement capability most of your peers do not have yet.

### Mid career (5-12 years)

**How this changes measurement:** The program KPIs you have built your reputation on, share of voice, coverage quality scores, media relationship metrics, still matter and should still be reported. What changes is what you add alongside them. AI share of voice, the frequency with which a brand appears in AI-generated answers about its category, is becoming a meaningful KPI. Citation persistence, whether coverage from a campaign stays citable in AI answers over time, is the measurement that tells you whether the owned infrastructure is working. Adding these metrics to a quarterly readout is not replacing the traditional ones. It is future-proofing the program's ability to demonstrate value as the discovery landscape continues to shift.

**How this changes pitching:** A pitch that generates traditional coverage and GEO-structured placement is worth more than a pitch that generates only traditional coverage. This means briefing journalists with attributed data, structured executive quotes, and original research that they can cite with attribution, not because reporters need the help, but because the article they write will be more durable in the AI-citation layer if it has those structural properties. This is a subtle shift in how pitch materials are prepared, not a wholesale change in how media relations works.

**How this changes program design:** The owned content calendar is no longer just a way to fill the editorial pipeline between campaign moments. It is the corpus that determines whether the campaign coverage has a durable foundation to rest on. Programs that treat owned content as filler and earned coverage as the "real work" may find that their earned coverage decays faster than programs that maintain a structured, updated owned foundation.

### Senior and executive (12+ years)

**The structural decision:** The aggregated market is asking senior communications leaders to make a budget decision with no obvious line item: how much of the PR program's investment should go toward building and maintaining the owned infrastructure that makes earned coverage durable, versus the traditional campaign investment that generates coverage in the first place? This is not a question that has a universal answer. It is a question that requires understanding the compounding return model, the idea that owned infrastructure investment tends to pay out over a longer horizon than campaign investment, and framing that model credibly for a CFO or client.

**The Hourglass context:** The Hourglass talent model (from the MarCom OS blueprint) is directly relevant here. Senior expert generalists who understand GEO as a PR discipline and can execute owned-corpus strategy alongside traditional media relations are exactly the profile the Hourglass top represents. The aggregated market is one of the clearest illustrations of why that profile is more durable than a team organized around high-volume campaign production.

**The conversation with leadership:** "AI is changing how people find things" is a statement that most leadership teams have already discounted as a technology story. The more useful frame is: "Our coverage is still generating clips, but the traffic-per-clip is declining. The brands that are holding their traffic-per-clip are the ones maintaining a structured owned knowledge base alongside their earned coverage program. Here is what that would cost, and here is the compounding return model." That is a budget conversation, not a technology conversation. This guide aims to give the senior professional the language to have it.

## Section 3: Compounding Cohort Decay: The PR/Comms Version

### EL5 explanation (explain like the reader is 10)

Imagine you are a lemonade stand, and every time you hand someone a cup, that person remembers your lemonade and tells their friends. That is your coverage: the moment people remember you and pass it on. Now imagine there is a machine that summarizes everything in the neighborhood and tells people "there is a lemonade stand on Oak Street" without sending them to your stand. They know you exist, but they never visit. And because they never visit, they do not tell their friends their own version of your lemonade story. Your coverage exists. Your audience does not show up. Every week that passes without fresh lemonade news, the machine's summary gets older and your name appears less often. This is compounding cohort decay in the aggregated market: the audience you built from a coverage wave fades, the coverage's citability fades, and unless you keep adding fresh, structured, citable content to the machine's memory, you gradually stop existing in the answers it gives.

### What a "cohort" means in PR/comms terms

In PR, a cohort is the audience generated by a specific coverage wave or campaign moment. The journalists who wrote about the brand during a product launch. The readers who encountered the brand's name in a story about the category. The researchers who added the brand to their mental model of the space. Each of those cohorts decays: reporters move beats, readers forget, category narratives evolve. The PR team's traditional job is to refresh those cohorts through ongoing coverage activity.

In the aggregated market, the cohort concept extends to AI systems. An AI model's training and retrieval behavior effectively incorporates a "cohort" of sources it treats as credible references for a brand. That cohort decays too: as fresher, more structured, more frequently-cited sources appear from competitors, the brand's representation in AI answers tends to decline unless the owned corpus is actively maintained.

### The three decay patterns

**Coverage Half-Life**

What it is: The period during which a piece of earned coverage actively generates discovery, visits, and citations. In traditional media, half-life is limited by how quickly a story falls off the front page and fades in search rankings. In the aggregated market, half-life has a second limiter: AI systems can summarize coverage without returning traffic to it, which means a clip can be "known to the system" while generating no audience contact.

How it compounds: A campaign that earns 200 clips in a week generates a peak of AI-citability during that period. If no fresh owned content exists to anchor the campaign's narrative after the clip wave subsides, the AI citation value tends to decay toward the brand's pre-campaign baseline. The clips remain in the media record. The AI answer layer gradually reassigns its citability weight to fresher sources.

Real-world illustration: A technology brand announces a research report, earns significant coverage during the launch week, and then publishes no structured follow-up content for three months. A competitor publishes a follow-up report with consistent headings, attributed statistics, and regular updates two months later. By month four, the competitor's research is appearing more frequently in AI answers about the category. The original brand's coverage still exists; the citation advantage has migrated.

Manifestation across market types: In the aggregated market, Coverage Half-Life is primarily a function of freshness and structure. In decentralized markets, it is a function of community activity and protocol participation recency. In autonomous markets, it is a function of API freshness and agent-callable interface availability. The decay mechanism is the same; the measurement differs.

**Relationship Attrition**

What it is: The decay of journalist and editor relationships when they are not maintained through consistent, substantive contact. In traditional PR, relationship attrition is an acknowledged risk managed through regular briefings, exclusives, and genuine reporter service. In the aggregated market, relationship attrition has an added dimension: the journalists themselves are navigating the same AI-intermediation pressure. Their own publications are losing referral traffic. Their editors are asking different questions about which stories generate lasting audience value.

How it compounds: A brand that builds strong media relationships during a news moment and then goes quiet for two quarters tends to find that the relationship has softened when it needs to re-engage. In the aggregated market, there is an additional compounding factor: the journalists whose relationships remain strongest with a brand are those who have found the brand's spokespeople and owned content to be genuinely useful sources. The brands that provide structured, attributable, verifiable information tend to maintain stronger journalist relationships than those that provide only narrative and quotes.

Real-world illustration: A brand's communications team builds strong relationships with two reporters covering the AI-in-marketing space during a product launch. Over the following six months, the team focuses on a social media campaign and does not maintain contact with the reporters. The reporters, covering an active space, develop stronger relationships with competitors who are publishing regular original research and providing consistent structured briefings. When the brand needs to re-enter the AI-in-marketing coverage cycle, it is effectively starting from a weaker relationship position than it was in at launch.

Manifestation across market types: In aggregated markets, Relationship Attrition primarily affects journalist and editor relationships. In decentralized markets, it affects core contributor and governance participant relationships. In autonomous markets, it affects the "relationship" between the brand's machine-readable presence and agent retrieval behavior. The human-relationship decay in aggregated markets is the most directly manageable through traditional PR investment.

**Share-of-Voice Erosion**

What it is: The gradual decline in a brand's presence in the ongoing media conversation about its category, relative to competitors. In traditional PR measurement, share of voice is measured in publication mentions versus a defined competitor set. In the aggregated market, share of voice has a second dimension: AI share of voice, measured in how often the brand appears in AI-generated answers about the category.

How it compounds: Traditional share of voice can be maintained through consistent coverage activity even without an owned corpus investment. AI share of voice tends to be more structurally determined: the brands with higher AI share of voice tend to be those with more structured, more frequently-updated, and more widely-cited owned content, not necessarily those with the highest traditional clip volume. A brand that focuses budget on campaign-burst coverage without maintaining a structured owned corpus may find its AI share of voice declining even as its traditional share of voice holds.

Real-world illustration: A consumer technology brand has consistently high traditional share of voice in its category, generated through a consistent earned media program. A newer competitor enters the category with a less prominent earned media presence but a well-maintained structured knowledge base, regular research publications with attributed statistics, and an FAQ architecture that AI systems can easily retrieve. Within two years, the competitor's AI share of voice in category queries approaches the incumbent's traditional share of voice in traditional media, despite the incumbent's significantly larger clip volume.

Manifestation across market types: In aggregated markets, Share-of-Voice Erosion operates at editorial cycle speed. In autonomous markets, it operates at algorithm speed, and recovery requires structural changes to machine-readable presence rather than just increased coverage activity.

## Section 4: How to Model It: Core Dynamics for the Aggregated Market

> **Writer note:** This section discusses planning frameworks, not financial projections. Include a clear disclaimer that nothing here is financial or legal advice, and that all budget framing is offered as a narrative planning model only.

> **Disclaimer language to include at the top of this section:** The frameworks below are planning tools for communications professionals, not financial projections. They offer a narrative model for discussing investment tradeoffs with clients and leadership. Nothing in this section is legal, financial, or compliance advice. Actual returns vary by organization, market, and execution.

### Module 1: The Citation Surface

**EL5 first:** The Citation Surface is everything your brand has published that a computer can find, understand, and repeat accurately when someone asks a question about your space. Think of it like a library card catalog for your brand's knowledge. If the catalog is well-organized, up to date, and has a good description of every book, the librarian (the AI) can find the right book quickly and tell the reader about it. If the catalog is messy, outdated, and full of books with no description, the librarian will use someone else's catalog instead.

**Professional depth:** The Citation Surface is the total body of owned and earned content that meets the structural criteria for AI citability: fresh publication or update dates, attributed statistics, named quotes, sequential headings, schema markup, consistent brand terminology. It is measurable (through citation-tracking tools and GEO audits) and improvable through deliberate editorial investment.

What drives it: Regular owned content publication with GEO-optimized structure; earned coverage that includes attributed statistics and quotes; external mentions and third-party citations that corroborate the owned corpus.

What decays it: Content aging without updates; coverage that is comprehensive in narrative but thin on citable structure; competitor corpus investment that draws citation weight away from the brand's content.

What a PR team can own vs. rent vs. measure: The PR team owns the editorial calendar and content structure of owned assets. It rents the discovery layer (no brand controls AI summarization). It can measure citation persistence, AI share of voice, and owned-corpus citability through existing and emerging tools.

**Worked example:** Q: My client's CEO just gave a major keynote and earned 200 clips. How does the aggregated market change what I do next? A: The 200 clips represent a peak in the brand's Citation Surface, but that peak tends to decay over four to eight weeks as fresher content appears. The traditional PR response is to maintain momentum with follow-up coverage activity. The aggregated market adds a step: within two weeks of the keynote, publish a structured summary of the CEO's key claims on the owned newsroom, with attributed statistics, clear headings, and a consistent update schedule. This anchors the keynote's citability in the owned corpus rather than letting it exist only in the third-party coverage, which the AI system may not cite at the same rate as a well-maintained owned source.

### Module 2: The Decay Clock

**EL5 first:** The Decay Clock is how fast your brand fades from the machine's memory if you stop adding fresh, well-organized content. Imagine you are building a sandcastle on the beach. Every day you do not add sand and fix the walls, the tide moves a little closer and waves knock a little more down. The coverage you earned is the castle you built. The Decay Clock is the tide. The owned corpus investment is the fresh sand that keeps the castle standing.

**Professional depth:** The Decay Clock is the rate at which a brand's AI citation presence decays in the absence of fresh owned content and ongoing earned coverage. AirOps research suggests pages not updated quarterly are approximately three times more likely to lose citations. The clock runs independently of traditional PR measurement: a brand can hold its traditional share of voice while its AI citation presence decays, because the two are influenced by different factors.

What drives it: Content aging without updates; competitor corpus growth that draws citation weight; AI model updates that reassign credibility signals based on current data.

What decays it faster: Sporadic publication schedules; content that is narrative-rich but structure-poor; owned assets that are not updated to reflect current brand positions.

What a PR team can own: The update schedule for owned assets. The team controls the input to the Decay Clock through editorial calendar management.

**Worked example:** Q: My client has 500 pieces of owned content on their website, but their AI share of voice has been declining for three quarters. What should I look for? A: The first question is freshness. Content that has not been updated in twelve or more months tends to lose AI citation weight even if it was once well-cited. The second question is structure: even excellent content may be invisible to AI systems if it lacks consistent headings, attributed statistics, and schema markup. A GEO audit, which evaluates owned content against AI citability criteria, tends to identify a prioritized list of high-value pages that, once updated and structured, can recover AI citation presence more efficiently than a wholesale content redesign.

### Module 3: The Owned Corpus

**EL5 first:** The Owned Corpus is your brand's library, specifically the part of the library that your brand built and controls. It is the place where the machine goes to check what your brand actually says, believes, and knows. If your library is well-organized, accurate, and up to date, the machine will cite it. If it is disorganized, outdated, or thin, the machine will cite someone else's library and your brand will be summarized, or absent, in the answer.

**Professional depth:** The Owned Corpus is the structured body of first-party content that the brand controls: the newsroom, the executive commentary archive, the research publication record, the FAQ and knowledge base. In the aggregated market, the Owned Corpus functions as the evidentiary foundation that gives earned coverage a durable anchor. Coverage that has a well-maintained Owned Corpus behind it tends to remain more citable over time than coverage with no owned support structure.

What drives it: Deliberate editorial investment in structured, attributed, fresh owned content; original research with cited sources; FAQ architecture that answers the specific questions AI systems are asked about the brand's category.

What a PR team can own vs. rent vs. measure: The PR team owns the Owned Corpus entirely. It is the one asset in the aggregated market that does not depend on a third-party platform's goodwill. It can be measured through GEO audits, citation-tracking tools, and AI share of voice monitoring.

**Budget modeling guidance (note: planning framework only, not financial advice):**

The Attrition Tax: The ongoing budget cost of recreating media momentum from scratch when each campaign's coverage decays without an owned corpus to anchor it. The question to ask clients and leadership is: "How much of our current PR budget is spent rebuilding from zero each campaign, versus compounding on an existing foundation?"

The Compounding Return on Owned Corpus Investment: A well-maintained Owned Corpus tends to accumulate citability over time as it gathers external mentions, journalist references, and AI citation history. Unlike a campaign's coverage, the Owned Corpus does not reset to zero at the end of a campaign cycle.

**Worked example:** Q: My client asks why they should invest in a structured newsroom when they are already investing in earned coverage. What do I say? A: The newsroom is what gives the earned coverage a longer shelf life. A story that runs in a major outlet and cites the brand's own research page, rather than only quoting a spokesperson, tends to be more durable in AI citation because the AI has a direct, updateable source to link back to. Without the newsroom, every campaign starts from zero. With it, each campaign's coverage adds to a growing citation foundation. The question is not whether to invest in earned coverage; it is whether the investment compounds or resets each time.

### Module 4: The AI Share of Voice Dial

**EL5 first:** AI Share of Voice is how often your brand shows up when someone asks a computer a question about your space. Imagine there are 100 questions people ask about your industry every day. If your brand appears in the answer to 12 of them and your biggest competitor appears in 30, your AI share of voice is 12 out of whatever the total is. The goal is not to be in every answer. It is to be in the answers that matter and to keep your position from eroding as the competition invests in theirs.

**Professional depth:** AI Share of Voice is the PR profession's emerging analog to search engine ranking. It is measurable through query testing, emerging SaaS tools, and manual brand monitoring. It correlates with owned corpus quality, freshness, and external citation volume more than with traditional clip count. It is influenced by incumbency bias (established brands tend to appear more often in AI answers) and by the quality of third-party corroboration (mentions in trusted sources correlate more strongly with AI visibility than backlinks).

## Section 5: Common Questions Answered

**Q1: What do I tell my client when they say clip count is still the number that matters to them?**

Clip count still matters, and it is not going away as a KPI. The more honest version of the conversation is that clip count is measuring content creation and placement, which remain valuable. What it may not be measuring accurately is audience contact, because a growing share of searches that would have driven a click to the covered story now end in an AI summary. Adding one additional metric alongside clip count, something like referral traffic from covered stories, or an AI share of voice spot check, tends to give the picture more depth without requiring a wholesale measurement overhaul. The goal is not to replace the client's measurement model; it is to future-proof it.

**Q2: How do I explain to a reporter why I am asking them to include a specific data point with attribution?**

You do not need to mention AI at all. The traditional case for including attributed data in coverage is accuracy and credibility: a story that cites a specific figure from a named source is harder to dispute and more useful to readers. The aggregated market adds an additional benefit without changing the ask. A well-sourced, statistic-rich article also tends to perform better in AI-generated summaries because the data points are more citable. The pitch technique stays the same. The benefit is larger than it used to be.

**Q3: My client's Wikipedia page is inaccurate. Is that an AI problem now, too?**

Yes, in a direct way. Wikipedia was the second-most-cited domain in Semrush's analysis of AI citation patterns, capturing over 26% of citations across a large sample. AI systems frequently draw brand descriptions, executive names, and product claims from Wikipedia entries. An inaccurate Wikipedia page is both a traditional reputation risk and an AI-citation risk: the AI may repeat the inaccuracy in its answers. Wikipedia reputation management, which many PR teams have treated as a secondary task, tends to deserve more structured attention in the aggregated market.

**Q4: How do I explain AI share of voice to a CFO who is asking why we are spending on content infrastructure?**

The CFO-facing framing is: AI share of voice is to AI-mediated discovery what organic search ranking was to search-engine-mediated discovery. We built organic search presence through SEO investment over time. We build AI share of voice through owned corpus investment: structured, fresh, well-cited content that meets the criteria AI systems use to select citations. The difference is that AI share of voice is early enough that a brand can build a defensible position before the space becomes as competitive as organic search. The cost of building it now tends to be significantly lower than the cost of recovering from a fully competitive landscape in three years. This is a planning model, not a financial projection, and actual returns vary by market and execution.

**Q5: What does GEO actually mean for my day-to-day work? Is it replacing SEO?**

GEO (Generative Engine Optimization) is the discipline of making content citable by AI answer engines, the way SEO was the discipline of making content rankable by search engines. It does not replace SEO; it extends it. The structural properties that tend to improve GEO performance overlap significantly with good editorial practice: attributed statistics, clear headings, named quotes, frequent updates, original research. The PR professional's role in a GEO program tends to be familiar work done with a more explicit eye toward machine readability: ensuring that the press room is updated, that executive commentary has consistent structure, and that coverage pitches are designed to generate attributable, citable content rather than only narrative placement.

**Q6: A competitor of ours appears in almost every AI answer about our category. What drives that, and what can we do?**

Research suggests that AI models carry a strong incumbency bias: well-known brands with established citation records tend to appear more frequently in AI answers, even when competitors have comparable products. This advantage tends to diminish once a competitor earns a small but measurable reputation advantage in a specific area. The practical response is to identify the specific category queries where the competitor dominates and to build owned and earned content specifically structured around those queries: original research on the relevant topics, expert commentary with consistent attribution, and earned coverage that generates citable statistics. The bias can be reduced through sustained corpus investment rather than by trying to win in a head-to-head coverage volume race.

**Q7: Is the aggregated market shift permanent, or will it reverse as AI tools evolve?**

The evidence suggests the structural shift is durable, though the pace of change is uneven. The direction of travel, AI answer engines mediating a growing share of discovery queries, with fewer clicks returning to original sources, is documented across multiple independent measurement sources. The specific percentages (60% zero-click searches, 33% traffic decline for publishers) will shift as the technology and user behavior evolve. The underlying mechanism, an aggregating layer that owns the demand relationship and returns very little of it to the original sources, has precedent in every prior generation of platform development. The strategic response, building owned assets that do not depend on the aggregator's goodwill for their value, tends to be more durable than the tactics optimized for any specific platform's current algorithm.

## Section 6: Takeaways

**Takeaway 1:** Stop counting only clips. Start measuring citation persistence.

The PR program that reports only clip count is measuring its inputs, not its outcomes. In the aggregated market, a clip that generates no lasting AI citation presence is a shorter-duration asset than a clip that anchors a durable owned corpus reference. Add one AI share of voice check to your quarterly measurement readout: run five to ten key category queries in a major AI assistant and count how often the brand appears. Do the same for your top two competitors. The gap between your traditional share of voice and your AI share of voice tells you where the corpus investment should go first.

**Takeaway 2:** Treat the press room like a product, not a filing cabinet.

The owned newsroom is the PR program's most underinvested asset in the aggregated market. A press room that is updated quarterly, organized with clear headings, and structured around the specific questions AI systems ask about the brand's category is a compounding citation asset. A press room that is updated only when there is news and organized for human browsing is an artifact that the AI will deprioritize as competing, fresher sources appear. Assign ownership of the press room's update schedule the same way you would assign ownership of a client relationship: someone is accountable, there is a calendar, and there is a measurable output.

**Takeaway 3:** Position owned corpus investment as a retention hedge, not a technology experiment.

The CFO-facing case for owned content infrastructure is not "AI is changing things." It is "the cost of rebuilding our coverage presence from zero every campaign is the Attrition Tax we are already paying, and we are not getting the compounding return that a maintained owned foundation would generate." Frame the investment as a hedge against the coverage decay that every campaign already experiences, rather than as a bet on an AI trend. That framing tends to land with leadership teams who are skeptical of technology predictions and persuaded by budget logic.
